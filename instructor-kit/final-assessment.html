Build It, Then Teach It

The end-of-course celebration, which is also the assessment. Each participant shows two things. They built a real game with their child (Star Catcher, plus one extension of their own), and they can teach a five-minute mini-lesson the way the course coaches it. This isn't a test to pass or fail. It's a bar to reach, with as many tries as it takes, and reaching it earns the certificate.

Covers Modules 1–5 Part A: Star Catcher + extension Part B: Teach-It demo Outcome: Complete / not yet
Why it's framed as a celebration

Participants are parents and caregivers, many of whom arrived unsure whether they were "techy enough." A grade would bring back exactly the fear the course works to remove. So there are no scores out of 100, no ranking, and nothing shared publicly except what they choose to show. The rubric tells you whether they've met the bar and tells them what to try next. Every criterion has a "not yet," never a "fail."

Part A · The Project

At Family Build Night (Session 5), each participant and their child build Star Catcher from lessons 4.3–4.4. The child directs and the parent types only when asked. Before or during Session 6, the participant adds one personal extension (alone, or better, with their child) and brings the project to the showcase.

Required deliverables
  • A saved Star Catcher project (in a Scratch account or as an .sb3 file) that passes the lesson 4.4 Project Completion checklist.
  • One personal extension from the Someday List or their own idea: stars speed up, rocks to dodge, a high score, levels, a new sprite or sound, or anything else they dream up.
  • The child's Game Design Card (Session 4 homework), or a quick one filled in at Session 5.
Star Catcher must…
  • Move the Bowl left and right with the arrow keys (events + loops + conditionals)
  • Keep score and lives in variables
  • Drop stars as clones from random spots, with the original hidden
  • Reset through a My Block (reset game)
  • End with a game over broadcast: GAME OVER shows and everything stops
  • Start a fresh game each time the green flag is pressed
The reference
  • Finished game: labs/module-4/l4-4-star-catcher-final.sb3
  • Rescue file (smallest playable version): labs/module-4/l4-3-star-catcher-part1.sb3
  • A family that used the rescue file is still on track. Finishing matters more than starting from blank.
  • The extension doesn't have to be big. It has to be theirs, and it has to work.

Part B · The Teach-It Demonstration

Each participant coaches a peer playing a child through a 5-minute mini-lesson taken from the course. It's the same format as every Teach-It role-play they've practised since Session 1, so nothing here should be new.

How it runs
  1. The participant picks a mini-lesson from any module, for example the 60-second editor tour (1.2), a repeat loop walk (2.2), arrow-key controls (2.3), fixing one Bug Hunt bug (3.3), or a first clone (4.1).
  2. The partner draws a kid card (labs/module-5/role-play-cards.html) and plays that child: age, mood, and habits.
  3. The "child" holds the mouse the whole time. The coach may point and ask, but may not touch it.
  4. Five minutes on a timer, then swap roles. You or a third participant observes and marks the teaching rubric.
When to run it
  • Easiest: during the Session 6 hands-off coaching rounds (lesson 5.1). Stretch each round from 4 to 5 minutes and observe one pair per round.
  • Or observe any Teach-It role-play from Sessions 2–6. A strong round in Session 3 counts.
  • Or, for a participant who missed Session 6, a 10-minute catch-up with you as the "kid."
  • Plant one bug on the kid card ("your cat won't stop moving") so every demo includes a stuck moment.
Observe kindly

Being watched while teaching is nerve-racking, especially for someone who learned Scratch five weeks ago. Sit beside the pair, not across from them. Keep the rubric on your lap instead of a clipboard in the air, and open your feedback with what went well. If a demo goes badly, it's a practice round: offer another one.

Rubric

Mark each criterion Not yet (1), Met (2), or Shining (3). A participant is complete when every criterion is Met (2) or better. There is no total to reach. "Shining" is there for you to celebrate, not to rank.

Uses the big ideas /3 One or more is missing: events, loops, conditionals, variables, clones, or a My Block. All six appear and do their job: green flag + broadcast, forever, if, score/lives, star clones, reset game. The participant can point to each one and say in plain words what it does.
Personal extension /3 No extension yet, or the extension breaks the original game. One extension added and working (speed-up, rocks, high score, levels, or their own idea). The extension came from the child's Someday List, or goes beyond the course's four suggestions.
Organization /3 Sprites are still "Sprite1"/"Sprite2", or there are stray unattached blocks and duplicate scripts. Sprites are named (Bowl, Star, Game Over), there are no stray blocks, and the project is saved and can be found again. Scripts are tidy (right-click → Clean up Blocks) and a comment or two explains the tricky parts.
Part B · The Teach-It demonstration
Asks, doesn't instruct /3 Mostly gives step-by-step orders ("Drag the blue one. Now click it."). Mostly guiding questions: "What do you think will happen?" "Where might that block live?" Questions shrink or grow to fit the "child" (the least help that unblocks), then fade away.
Lets the child drive /3 Grabs the mouse, or points so precisely that it amounts to the same thing. Hands stay off the mouse. The "child" makes the clicks and choices. Holds back through a real struggle and lets the "child" own the breakthrough.
Handles a bug calmly /3 Fixes the bug for them, or signals frustration ("No, not like that…"). Stays calm and uses the debugging steps: watch it, predict, change one thing, test. Frames the bug as good news ("Ooh, a mystery!") and the "child" finds the fix.
Age-appropriate /3 Language or pace ignores the kid card (jargon for a 6-year-old, hand-holding for a 12-year-old). Words, pace, and challenge fit the card's age and mood. Adapts on the fly when the "child" gets bored, silly, or upset.
Ends on a win /3 Time runs out mid-struggle, or the round ends with the coach's fix. The "child" gets something working and hears process praise ("You tested it after every change!"). Adds a "yet" and a next step: "You can't make it faster yet. Want to try that next time?"
Completion policy

A participant completes the course, and receives the certificate at the Session 6 graduation, when every criterion on the rubric is Met (2) or better. Anyone who isn't there yet gets one specific, kind next step per "Not yet" and another go: finish the extension at home and email the .sb3, or re-run the Teach-It demo with you. Nobody is held back from the showcase. Children present their games whatever the rubric says.
