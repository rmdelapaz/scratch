Module 1 — Getting Started with Scratch

How to run the first session: settle a room of nervous grown-ups, get everyone into the Scratch editor (account or not), tour it as a game, and end with every parent's cat walking and talking. The goal for Session 1 is confidence, not coverage.

3 lessons 2 hrs teaching Lessons 1.1–1.3 Level Beginner Maps to Session S1

How to Use This Guide

  • Same rhythm every lesson: Frame → Learn It (live demo) → Lab → Teach-It role-play → Check. Participants learn it themselves first, then practice coaching a "child" played by their partner.
  • Session 1 is mostly about fear. Your participants are adults with zero coding experience; many haven't been a "student" in decades. Every minute you spend making them feel safe here buys you an hour of attention later.
  • Demo on the projector, then hands on. Never ask a room to follow along while you talk fast. Show it once, slowly; then they do it while you circulate.
  • The workbook mirrors this guide. Point participants to the matching page before each lab so the steps are in front of them.
  • Teal = hands-on, amber = watch-outs. SAY: boxes are lines to read aloud, or for participants to use when coaching.
The through-line

Module 1 runs from "What is this?" to "I made that." Lesson 1.1 gives participants three words (sprite, stage, blocks) and one mindset ("…yet"). Lesson 1.2 gives them a map of the editor. Lesson 1.3 gives them their first working program, a cat that walks and says hello. Each lesson also rehearses one parenting move: the pitch (1.1), the 60-second tour (1.2) and coaching with questions (1.3). Every parent should leave with a saved project and a first journal entry.

Before Session 1 — Room & Account Logistics

One week before
  • Test the network from the actual room. Open scratch.mit.edu → Create. The page loading isn't enough: check that the cat appears and that the sprite library (cat "+" button) shows pictures. School and library filters often allow the site but block its asset servers, so the editor opens with no cat.
  • If anything is blocked: ask IT to allow *.scratch.mit.edu. If they can't do it in time, install the free Scratch app (scratch.mit.edu/download, for Windows, macOS, ChromeOS and Android tablets) on the room machines. Also bring a USB stick with the installers.
  • Send the "before you come" note: bring a laptop and charger if you have one; bring a mouse; bring reading glasses; no account needed, but if you want one, sign up at home and confirm the email first.
Room kit
  • USB mice, at least one per three participants. Trackpad drag-and-drop is the #1 physical barrier for adults.
  • Seats in pairs (role-plays start in Lesson 1.1). Put the projector where everyone can see it without twisting round.
  • Printed workbooks, pens, name tents.
  • Printed kid cards for the three role-plays in this guide (cut apart, one set per pair).
  • A sticky note of the Wi-Fi password on every table.
Accounts — what to tell the room

Accounts are optional in Module 1. Anyone can click Create and build without signing in. The only difference is saving: without an account, use File → Save to your computer (downloads an .sb3 file); with one, File → Save now saves to the cloud.

Parent-email confirmation. When someone joins, Scratch sends a confirmation email: to the adult's own address, or to a parent/guardian's address for a child's account. Until the link in that email is clicked, the account can save but can't share or comment. If someone signs up in class, have them confirm on their phone before they leave. Warn that the email sometimes lands in spam or promotions.

Kid accounts are homework, not class work. Parents create them at home, with their child, using their own email as the guardian address and a username that doesn't reveal the child's real name. Sharing safety is covered properly in Lesson 5.2 (Session 6).

Organizations: a school or library running many kids' accounts can apply for a free Scratch Teacher Account, which creates student accounts without individual emails. Mention it if a teacher asks, but don't set one up during the session.

Icebreaker: "Why are you here?" (5 min)

This is the Lesson 1.1 "first journal entry" said out loud. It does two jobs: it tells you who's in the room (child ages, hopes, worries), and it lets nervous adults hear that the person next to them is nervous too. In pairs, each person gets 90 seconds to answer one of these:

  • Why do you want to learn this? (a curious kid, a school requirement, your own itch)
  • What do you hope to make with your child? (a game, a dancing birthday card, a story starring the dog)
  • What worries you about it? (not techy enough, a frustrated child, screen time)

Model it first with a real, slightly vulnerable answer of your own. When volunteers share worries, write them on a flip-chart and keep it up all series. In Session 6 you'll cross them off together. Don't try to solve screen-time guilt now; say "Great one. We'll come back to that in Module 5."

Lesson Plans

1.1

What Is Scratch & Why Kids Love It

25 min
Objectives
  • Explain, in one sentence, what Scratch is
  • Describe how block-based coding differs from typed code
  • List three reasons Scratch is great for young learners
  • Introduce Scratch to their child with confidence
Teaching sequence
  1. The one-sentence definition (3m). Put it on the slide and have the room read it aloud together: "Scratch is a free website where kids make their own games, animations and stories by snapping together colorful blocks instead of typing code." Made by the MIT Media Lab, run by the non-profit Scratch Foundation, available in 70+ languages, no ads, no in-app purchases.
  2. Three words (3m). Point at the projector: sprite (the cat, a character or object), stage (the screen where the action happens), blocks (the instructions you snap together). "Learn these three and you can already follow along with your child."
  3. Blocks vs typing, live (5m). Script: "In typed code, one missing colon and nothing works. Watch." Show the Python-style snippet on the slide. Then build when ⚑ clicked → move 10 steps → say Hello! for 2 seconds in about 20 seconds. "Blocks only snap together if they make sense, so there's nothing to misspell." Then try to force a hat block under a move block to show that it won't go.
  4. Color = category (2m). Blue Motion, purple Looks, pink Sound, gold Events, orange Control, light-blue Sensing, green Operators, dark-orange Variables. "Kids read a program by its colors before they can read the words."
  5. What kids feel vs what they're learning (2m). "I made the cat dance!" = sequencing; "it keeps spinning forever" = loops; "when I press space it jumps" = events. "Your job isn't to lecture on these terms. Celebrate the moment, and now and then name it."
  6. Growth mindset: "yet" (2m). Look back at the worries flip-chart. "'I'm not a computer person' becomes 'I'm not a computer person yet.' Your kids learn how to handle frustration by watching you hit a bug and shrug."
Lab — Click around (folded into the demo)

No build file. During step 3, participants have the editor open and drag any one block into the middle and click it. That's the whole lab: prove to themselves that nothing breaks.

Circulate for: anyone frozen with hands off the mouse ("Go on, drag anything, you can't break it"); trackpad strugglers (give them a mouse now, not in Lesson 1.3).

Teach-It role-play — "The pitch" (6 min)

Partner A plays the child from the kid card; Partner B makes the pitch. No screen, just a conversation. Swap after 2 minutes, then debrief for 1 minute.

Kid card A — Maya, 9

Mood: Skeptical. Just got home, a bit tired. Typical behavior: "Coding is boring, it's just maths." Loves drawing and making up stories about the cat next door. Warms up if the idea is hers.

"Want to make your own game where you decide the rules? There's a free thing called Scratch where you snap colorful blocks together and a little cat does whatever you tell it. Want to try making it talk?"
"You like drawing. In Scratch you can draw your own character and make it move."
"I'm learning it too, so I'll probably get stuck. Maybe you can help me."

Debrief question: "Kids, which line made you curious? Which one sounded like school?" (It's almost always the word learn. "Make beats learn every time.")

Watch-outs
  • "I'm not a computer person." Answer it head-on: "If you can drag a block, you can help." Never say "this is easy". To someone who is struggling that sounds like "so you must be slow."
  • Comparing with faster peers. Some participants already have Scratch-mad kids and will race ahead. Give them the side job of "table helper", which turns speed into something useful for the room.
  • Screen-time guilt comes up early. Acknowledge it and park it: making things in Scratch is creative screen time, and Module 5 is about exactly this.
  • The community question. "Can strangers message my kid?" Scratch has a moderated community for sharing and comments, but Module 1 never touches it. Everything today stays private. Full coverage in Lesson 5.2.
  • ScratchJr confusion. Parents of 5–7-year-olds will ask. ScratchJr is a separate picture-based tablet app, covered in Lesson 5.3. For now, younger kids sit beside the parent.
Check — quiz

Answers: the biggest advantage of blocks is no syntax or typing errors that break the program (B); a block's color tells you its category (B); the best first-session goal is one "whoa, I made that!" moment (C); new: the three key words are sprite, stage, blocks, and the cat is a sprite (B).

1.2

A Guided Tour of the Scratch Editor

33 min
Objectives
  • Open the Scratch editor, with or without an account
  • Name the four main areas of the editor and what each does
  • Find any block using the colored category menu
  • Use the green flag and the stop button
  • Save their work, and know how to set up a safe kid account
Teaching sequence
  1. The map (4m). Show the four-zone slide, then the real editor. 1 Blocks Palette (left: pick instructions) → 2 Code Area (middle: snap them together) → 3 Stage (upper right: watch it happen) → 4 Sprite List (lower right: choose who you're coding). "Four zones. Learn these and you've learned the editor."
  2. "Point and Name" game (10m). See the box below. It turns the tour into a game so nobody has to sit through a lecture.
  3. Green flag & red stop (2m). Flag = run every when ⚑ clicked script. Stop = halt everything. "Your child will press stop a lot, and that's healthy experimenting." Show the small / large / full-screen stage buttons.
  4. The #1 beginner trap (2m). Live: add the Scratch-library "Dog2" (or any) sprite, select it, and show that the cat's code seems to "vanish". "Code belongs to whichever sprite is selected. Nine times out of ten, 'my code isn't working' means the wrong sprite is selected."
  5. — Break (10m) —
  6. Code / Costumes / Sounds tabs (2m). Click the cat's Costumes tab: two walking costumes. "Switching costumes is how animation works. We'll use that in Module 2."
  7. Saving, for real (4m). Everyone does File → Save to your computer, finds the .sb3 in Downloads, then File → Load from your computer to reopen it. Anyone signed in also uses File → Save now. Point out that the offline Scratch app uses the same File menu, so files move freely between the app and the website.
  8. Kid-account walkthrough (2m, on screen only). Click Join Scratch and walk through it without submitting: username that doesn't reveal the real name, password you both can find, birth month/year, parent/guardian email, then the confirmation email. "Do this at home with your child."
"Point and Name" game (10 min)

Round 1: you call, they point (3m). Call out a name and everyone points at the screen, finger on the glass. Go faster as you go: "Stage!" "Blocks palette!" "Green flag!" "Sprite list!" "The Looks category!" "The Costumes tab!" "The stop button!" "Where's the cat's name?" You can see at a glance who's lost, so walk over.

Round 2: you point, they name (3m). Point at zones on the projector and the room calls out the name. Then point at a colored dot and have them call the category and say one block it holds.

Round 3: in pairs, find it (4m). Partners take turns challenging each other: "Find a block that plays a sound." "Find the block that starts a program." (gold Events → when ⚑ clicked) "How many colors of dot are there?" (nine, including the pink My Blocks.) Whoever finds it drags it into the code area and clicks it once to see what it does.

Keep it light and competitive. When they're done, say: "That's exactly the game you'll play with your child."

Lab — Save & reload drill

No build file. Everyone leaves the break with a project saved as a .sb3 (or saved to their account) and has reopened it once. This habit matters more than any block.

Circulate for: files saved somewhere the person can't find again (walk them to Downloads); a shared library computer that wipes files on logout (have them email the .sb3 to themselves); account sign-ups waiting on an unconfirmed email.

Teach-It role-play — "The 60-second tour" (5 min)

Now at the screen. Partner A is the child and holds the mouse; Partner B gives the tour with hands in their lap. Swap once (2m each), then debrief for 1 minute.

Kid card B — Leo, 6

Mood: Excited, wiggly. Typical behavior: Can't read most of the block words yet. Clicks everything fast, including the cat, and loses interest in anything longer than a sentence. Loves pressing big buttons.

"See these four parts? Over here are the blocks. That's your toy box. In the middle is where you build. Up here is where the cat does what you say. And this green flag makes it GO. Want to press it?"
"How many colors of block can you find? Let's count the dots."
"Can you teach me the four parts?" (older kids)

Debrief question: "Coaches, did your hand go for the mouse? How many times?" Name the rule: hands in your lap. Point with a finger, ask a question, let them click. For a 6-year-old, use nicknames ("toy box", "GO button") and skip the vocabulary.

Watch-outs
  • Code on the wrong sprite. This is the classic mistake. From today on, it's the first thing to check whenever something "doesn't work".
  • "I deleted everything!" Usually the wrong sprite is selected. Otherwise, right-click the code area → Undo, or Edit → Restore Sprite for a deleted sprite. Show this calmly, once.
  • Browser zoom and small text. Adults squint at the tiny block text. Teach Ctrl/⌘ + to zoom the page, and the + / − magnifier buttons at the bottom-right of the code area.
  • The language globe. Someone will click it and switch to another language. It's the globe icon at top-left; switch back from there.
  • Home-device questions ("Will this run on our old iPad?"). Scratch runs in any modern browser, but a tablet's touch drag is fiddly and there's no app for iPad (use the browser). Offer to answer at the break so the room keeps moving.
  • Mixing up the website and the app. The editor is the same in both. Only saving differs: an account lives on the website; the app saves files.
Check — quiz

Answers: nothing happens when you run it → the code is on a different sprite from the one selected (B); the button that runs the project is the green flag (A); category colors help you find the kind of block you need in the palette (B); new: without an account, you keep your work with File → Save to your computer (C).

1.3

Your First Project: Make the Cat Move & Talk

27 min
Objectives
  • Drag, drop and snap blocks together to build a script
  • Start a program with the when ⚑ clicked event
  • Combine Motion and Looks blocks to make a sprite act
  • Test, tweak and troubleshoot a simple project
  • Coach their child through building it themselves
Teaching sequence — live demo script
  1. Show the destination (1m). Run the finished program from the reference file. "Three blocks. That's a complete, working program. Let's build it one block at a time." Everyone opens a fresh project with the cat selected in the sprite list.
  2. The hat (1m). "Gold Events. Drag when ⚑ clicked into the middle. It's called a hat because it sits on top and starts everything."
  3. Move (2m). "Blue Motion. Drag move 10 steps under the hat. Watch for the grey shadow, and let go when you see it." Deliberately drop one a little too far away and show that the flag does nothing. Then snap it and click the flag: the cat moves a tiny bit.
  4. Say (2m). "Purple Looks. Drag say Hello! for 2 seconds under the move block." Click the white oval and type Hello! I'm Scratch Cat. Click the 10 and change it to 50. Run it.
  5. Sequence (2m). "Scratch runs blocks top to bottom, one after another. That's called a sequence. Swap the order and the cat talks first, then walks." Drag the say block above move and run it to prove it. Then show the reset: add go to x: 0 y: 0 right after the hat so the cat stops marching off the edge.

Lab — Make the Cat Move & Talk (12 min)

Build the three-block script, run it, then pick "Make it yours" challenges from the workbook: change the message to a joke; change 50 to 200; add a second say; start sound Meow; change size by 25; turn ↻ 15 degrees; next costume for a step of walking. Finish by saving. Reference: labs/module-1/l1-3-cat-move-talk.sb3.

Circulate for: blocks that look attached but aren't (drag the hat and see whether everything comes with it); the cat piling up at the right edge; typing into the wrong oval; a participant who's done in 3 minutes (give them the "walk and look like walking" challenge, then make them a table helper).

Teach-It role-play — "Treasure hunt, not dictation" (5 min)

Partner A is the child and drives; Partner B coaches the build using only questions, with no direct instructions like "drag the move block". Swap once, then debrief for 1 minute.

Kid card C — Sam, 11

Mood: Impatient and confident. Typical behavior: "Can we just make a game?" Clicks ahead before you finish talking. Types silly messages and tests the limits. Goes quiet and says "this is dumb" when a block won't snap.

"We want the cat to walk. Which color do you think has the moving blocks?"
"The message is yours. What should the cat say?"
"Hmm, it didn't work yet. Interesting! Why do you think it did that?"
"Can you make the cat cross the stage and say two things?" (goal, not steps, for age 12+)

Debrief question: "What did the coach do when the block wouldn't snap?" Praise any coach who stayed calm and asked a question. Remind them: the words, the number and the green flag belong to the child. End on a high: they show the project to someone.

Watch-outs
  • Trackpad drag. Holding a click while moving is hard on a trackpad, so blocks drop in the wrong place. Hand over a mouse. On a trackpad, suggest a firm press-and-hold, or three-finger drag on a Mac.
  • Clicking a block runs it. A single click on any block or stack runs it once, even without the flag. That's handy for testing, but it confuses people who expected the flag only.
  • Cat walks off-screen. Every run adds 50 more steps. Fix it by dragging the cat back, or with go to x: 0 y: 0 after the hat.
  • Upside-down cat after the turn challenge. Set rotation style to left-right in the sprite info (direction). It's covered properly later, so just fix it for them.
  • say vs say … for 2 seconds. The plain say block leaves the speech bubble up. The lesson uses the timed version.
  • "I'm so slow." Say it plainly to the room: dragging feels clumsy for the first hour for everyone, and by Lesson 2.2 it's automatic.
Check — quiz

Answers: top of the script → when ⚑ clicked (B); a stack runs top to bottom, in order (A); the cat moves but won't talk → is the "say" block snapped onto the stack? (B); new: swap move and say and the cat talks first, then walks (C).

Closing the Session

S1 wrap-up (10 min)

Journal (5m). Workbook journal prompts for 1.3, plus the confidence number from 1–10. Tell them to write it down, because they'll compare it in Session 6. Share (2m). Two or three people read one line. Try at home this week (2m): (1) have the "want to make your own game?" chat; (2) let your child build the cat program, hands in your lap; (3) give them a "coder's notebook"; (4) optional: create a kid account together and confirm the email. Last minute: everyone checks their .sb3 is saved where they can find it. Preview S2: "Next time the cat stops doing one thing and starts behaving, with timing, loops and choices."

Module 2 — Core Coding Concepts

How to teach the three ideas under every program ever written: sequences, loops, and conditionals. Each one starts away from the screen, with a game the adults can later play at home. By the end, parents have a walking cat, a cat they drive with the arrow keys, and words for explaining both to a child.

3 lessons ~90 min teaching Lessons 2.1–2.3 Level Beginner Maps to Session S2

How to Use This Guide

  • Every lesson follows the same shape: Unplugged (the idea off the screen) → Teach (a short live demo) → Lab (they build it) → Teach-It (pairs role-play teaching a child) → Check. Module 2 leans hardest on the unplugged part. The two off-screen games are what parents take home, so give them their full time.
  • Project the editor zoomed in. Use the + magnifier at the bottom-right of the code area (and browser zoom) so the back row can read block text. Tell the room they can zoom too; many will need to and won't ask.
  • Have mice ready. Nesting blocks inside the "mouth" of a C-shaped loop or if is the hardest drag in the course so far. On a trackpad it can feel impossible. Put a basket of USB mice on the front table.
  • Red stop sign, early and often. Before the first forever, show that the red stop above the stage ends everything. People who are afraid of breaking the computer relax once they've seen it.
  • Teal = hands-on, amber = watch-outs, purple = concept / Teach-It.
The through-line

One cat, three upgrades. It performs a timed greeting (sequence), walks across the stage with moving legs (loop), then obeys the arrow keys (forever + if). Name the pattern at the end of the session: "Steps in order, steps that repeat, steps that choose. Those three ideas are in every app on your phone." The arrow-key script comes back in Module 3's games, so ask everyone to save it.

Lesson Plans

2.1

Sequences & Timing

33 min
Objectives
  • Explain a sequence as steps carried out in order, top to bottom, followed literally
  • Show that the same blocks in a different order behave differently
  • Use wait (1) seconds (Control) for pacing, and know that say … for 2 seconds already pauses
  • Run the "robot game" with a child to teach sequencing away from the screen
Teaching sequence
  1. Unplugged warm-up: Program the Facilitator (10m). Stand on the far side of the room from the door. Say: "I'm a robot. I can only do exactly what you tell me, one instruction at a time. Your job: get me out that door." Follow every instruction literally. "Walk to the door" → "I don't know 'walk to'. Give me steps." "Take a step" → one tiny shuffle. "Turn" → "Which way? How far?" Let them run into your literalness a few times, then write their instructions on the whiteboard as a numbered list. Draw out three things as they come up:
    • Order matters. Swap "turn right" and "take 3 steps" on the board and walk the new version into a wall.
    • Precision matters. "Turn" needs a direction and an amount, the way turn 15 degrees has a number in it.
    • The loop seed. Someone will say "step, step, step, step…" Stop and ask: "Is there a shorter way to say that?" Someone offers "take 8 steps" or "keep walking until you reach the door." Write it down and circle it: "Hold that thought. That's next hour's lesson."
    End by opening the door and asking what went wrong along the way. Every answer is a bug they found and fixed. That was debugging.
  2. Order changes everything (4m). Live demo on a fresh project: when green flag clicked → move (100) steps → say (Made it!) for (2) seconds. Run it. Drag the say block above the move. Run again. "Same blocks, different order, different show. The computer did what we said, not what we meant. Nothing broke. It showed us exactly what our recipe says."
  3. The wait block (4m). Open the orange Control category; wait (1) seconds is at the top. Build the knock-knock joke without waits, run it, then add wait (1) seconds between lines. Point at the words "for 2 seconds" on the say block. That block holds by itself. Plain say doesn't. Launch the lab.
  4. Lab (10m) then Teach-It role-play (5m), both below.
Lab — Build a Greeting Routine

A four-beat welcome on the cat: when green flag clicked → go to x: (0) y: (0) → say (Hi, I'm Scratchy!) for (2) seconds → wait (1) seconds → move (120) steps → say (Welcome to my world!) for (2) seconds. Challenge: add start sound (Meow) and next costume between the wait and the move. Reference: labs/module-2/l2-1-greeting-routine.sb3.

Circulate for: blocks that look stacked but aren't snapped (a gap means that block never runs, so drag it closer until it clicks); a missing go to x: 0 y: 0 (the cat walks off the stage after two runs); leftover S1 scripts that also start on the green flag and fight the new one.

Teach-It role-play — the robot game

Pairs. One partner is the child and one is the parent. Hand the "child" the kid card. The parent asks the child to "program" them to make a peanut-butter sandwich (mime it), then follows each step hilariously literally. Swap roles for the last minute.

Kid card — Maya, age 6

Giggly, loves being in charge. Can't read "seconds" yet. Gives huge instructions ("make the sandwich!") and gets frustrated when the robot doesn't "just know." Wants to press the green flag again and again.

I'm a robot. I only do exactly what you say, one step at a time. What's my first step?
You said "put peanut butter on the bread," so I put the whole jar on the bread! How can we change that step?
The computer is a robot just like I was. If it looks wrong, let's read our steps in order and find the mix-up.

Debrief (30s): "What did the parent do when Maya got frustrated?" The answer to listen for: laughed with her, then shrank the step. Nobody said she was wrong.

Watch-outs
  • "My cat is frozen!" It's almost always a say … for 2 seconds holding, as designed. Read the block aloud with them. Don't let anyone restart their laptop over it.
  • Dragging from the middle of a stack. Grabbing a block takes every block below it along with it. That's a feature, but it surprises adults. Demo it once on purpose.
  • Deleting by accident. A block dropped back onto the palette disappears. Ctrl/Cmd + Z (or right-click the code area) brings it back. Say so before the lab.
  • Self-comparison. Faster neighbours finish the challenge while others are still snapping block 3. Say out loud: "The challenge is optional. Everyone's goal today is the six blocks."
  • "The robot game is babyish." Some adults feel silly playing it. Remind them they're rehearsing a game for their kid, not being tested on it. For older kids at home, flip the roles: the child is the robot and catches the adult's bugs.
Check — quiz

Answers: a sequence = A) steps carried out in order, one after another; wait lives in B) Control (orange); cat "freezes" 2 seconds = B) a say-for-2-seconds or wait block holding, as designed; say … for 2 seconds vs plain say = B) the timed one pauses the script before the next block; plain say moves straight on.

2.2

Loops — Making Things Repeat

27 min
Objectives
  • Use repeat (10) and forever (Control) and choose between them
  • Explain that only the blocks inside the C-shape repeat
  • Build a walking animation with move + next costume + wait inside a loop
  • Stop a runaway forever with the red stop sign
Teaching sequence
  1. The hard way first (3m). Point back at the circled line from the warm-up ("step, step, step…"). Drag in four move (10) steps blocks and pretend to reach for sixteen more. Groan. Then swap in repeat (20) with one move inside. "One loop replaces twenty blocks. And changing 20 to 100 takes one second."
  2. repeat vs forever (4m). Both are orange Control blocks shaped like a C. repeat = you know how many times. forever = until someone presses stop. Put a say (Done!) block after a forever loop and show that it never runs. Now press the red stop sign. "Forever means forever. The stop sign is always your way out, and you can't break anything with it."
  3. Inside vs outside the C (3m). Build the two comparison scripts: repeat 3 { say Ha! } vs repeat 3 { move 10 } then say Done!. Run both. Point at the mouth of the C: "Inside repeats, outside runs once." Show how to drop a block into the mouth so the C stretches to fit. Launch the lab.
  4. Lab (12m, after the break) then Teach-It role-play (5m).
Lab — A Real Walking Cat

when green flag clicked → go to x: (-200) y: (0) → repeat (20) { move (20) steps · next costume · wait (0.1) seconds }. The cat strides across with moving legs. Let them tune the wait to feel speed change. Challenge: wrap the walk in forever, add if on edge, bounce and set rotation style [left-right] so it patrols back and forth. Reference: labs/module-2/l2-2-walking-cat.sb3.

Circulate for: next costume or wait sitting below the loop instead of inside it (the cat glides with frozen legs, or zooms across in a blink); a missing go to start position; upside-down cats in the challenge (rotation style).

Teach-It role-play — spot the loop

Round 1 (2m): the "parent" plays spot-the-loop out loud. Round 2 (3m): the "child" has built a script with the same block copied many times. The parent may only ask questions and must not fix anything.

Kid card — Leo, age 9

Proud of his work. He's copy-pasted move (10) steps fifteen times and it works. He bristles at "that's wrong." He likes counting and races.

Brushing teeth is a loop: scrub, scrub, scrub, repeat until clean! Can you think of another loop?
That works! Is there a block that could do all of those for us at once, so we don't have to make so many?

Debrief: "Did Leo find the loop himself?" If the parent ended up dragging the block in for him, that's normal for a first try. Name it kindly and point ahead to 5.1, "The Art of Not Taking Over."

Watch-outs
  • Trackpad nesting. Dropping a block into the C mouth is a precise drag. If someone's block keeps landing below the loop, hand them a mouse before they conclude they're "not a computer person."
  • Fear of forever. Some adults won't press the green flag on a forever script. Stand beside them, click the red stop together once, and move on.
  • "The cat zooms across in a blink." The wait is missing or sits outside the loop. Put wait (0.1) inside and try 0.2 or 0.3.
  • Upside-down cat (challenge). By default a sprite rotates all the way around. Add set rotation style [left-right] at the top.
  • Break-time drift. Phones come out at the break and family logistics take over. Restart the lab on time with the script already on the projector. Latecomers can copy it without asking.
Check — quiz

Answers: blink exactly 5 times = A) repeat 5; only B) the blocks inside the C-shape repeat; a block after forever will A) never run; cat zooms across in a blink = A) add or lengthen a wait inside the loop.

2.3

Conditionals & Sensing — Making Choices

30 min
Objectives
  • Use if <> then and if <> then / else (Control) to make decisions
  • Read the hexagon Sensing blocks: touching (mouse-pointer)?, key (space) pressed?, mouse down?, touching color?
  • Combine forever + if, the "game loop," so a check runs constantly
  • Teach if/then through everyday rules and a drive-the-cat game
Teaching sequence
  1. Unplugged: "If it's raining, then…" (5m). Call-and-response. You say the "if," the room shouts the "then": "IF it's raining…" (umbrella!) "IF the light is red…" (stop!) "IF the baby is asleep…" (whisper!) Then add "otherwise": "IF it's raining, THEN umbrella, OTHERWISE…" (sunglasses!). Now each pair writes two rules from their own household on a sticky note: one if/then and one if/then/otherwise. Collect three or four and read them aloud. Finish with the point that matters: "Your kids already live by conditionals. Bedtime, screen time, dessert. Today we just give them the block." One follow-up question sets up the game loop: "How often do you check whether it's raining? Once in the morning, or all day?" The answer is all day, constantly. Hold on to that.
  2. if/then + Sensing (3m). Show if <touching (edge)?> then { say (Ouch!) for (1) seconds }. Point at the pointy hexagon slot: it only accepts a true/false question, and those come mostly from the light-blue Sensing category. Read it aloud as a sentence.
  3. forever + if, the game loop (3m). Build when green flag clicked → forever { if <touching (mouse-pointer)?> then { start sound (Meow) } }. Hover over the cat and it meows. Now pull the if out of the forever and run it again: it checks once at the start and never again. "Remember the rain question? All day. That's what forever does. It keeps asking."
  4. if/then/else (2m). Two mouths: top when true, bottom when false. Read it aloud with "otherwise." Kids find that friendlier than "else." Launch the lab.
  5. Lab (12m) then Teach-It role-play (5m).
Lab — Drive the Cat with Arrow Keys

when green flag clicked → forever { four if/then blocks: key (right arrow) pressed? → change x by (10); left arrow → change x by (-10); up arrow → change y by (10); down arrow → change y by (-10) }. Suggest building one arrow, testing it, then right-click → Duplicate the if block and change the dropdown and number. Challenge: "if touching (edge)?, say Ouch!" Reference: labs/module-2/l2-3-arrow-key-cat.sb3.

Circulate for: an if sitting below the forever instead of inside it; change x vs change y mixed up (left/right = x, up/down = y); the "wrong" sign on a number (−10 is left/down); stage without keyboard focus (they have to click the stage once).

Teach-It role-play — coach with questions

The "child" drives the mouse and keyboard. The "parent" keeps their hands in their lap and may only ask questions. Goal: one working arrow key.

Kid card — Sam, age 12

A little too cool for this: "Scratch is for little kids." Wants to make "a real game." Types fast, skips reading, and gets annoyed when the cat doesn't move. He's forgotten to click the stage.

How does the cat know you pressed a key? Which color has "senses"?
We want it to check all the time, not just once. What loop does that?
This forever-plus-if pattern is how real games work. Every game you play is checking things like this many times a second.

Debrief: "What made Sam lean in?" Usually it's hearing it connected to real games. For younger children (5–7), the lesson says a single if is plenty: "if touching the pointer, meow."

Watch-outs
  • "My cat won't move!" Check keyboard focus first: click the stage once. Next, check the nesting: change → inside if → inside forever.
  • Laptop arrow keys. Some small keyboards have tiny or shared arrow keys (Fn). If someone struggles to press them, let them pick other keys in the dropdown (for example w/a/s/d).
  • Three levels of nesting. Blocks inside blocks inside blocks is the first real "hold it in your head" moment, and it feels clumsy for almost everyone. Say so before they start. Praise the persistence out loud.
  • Home-device questions ("will this work on my kid's Chromebook / iPad?"). Park them on a "parking lot" sheet and answer at the break or after the session. Don't derail the lab.
  • Operators temptation. A keen participant asks about and/or. That's great for 12+, but save it. Operators arrive naturally in Lesson 3.2.
Check — quiz

Answers: what fits an "if ___ then" slot = A) a hexagon (true/false) block; games wrap ifs in forever B) so the condition is checked constantly, not just once; if/then/else is best when B) you want one thing if true and a different thing otherwise; correct script but cat won't move = A) click the stage so it has keyboard focus.

Teaching sequence

    go to x: -200 y: -100

A Simple Game with Variables & Score

32 min
Objectives
  • Explain a variable and create one (score, "For all sprites")
  • Use set [score] to 0 and change [score] by 1; show the score on the stage
  • Combine loops, if, sensing, and operators into a playable game
  • Coach a child to design a game on paper and avoid the scope trap
Teaching sequence
  1. The whiteboard (3m). Stick a note on the wall: "SCORE: 0." Pretend to catch something, cross it out, write 1.
    That's a variable: a labeled box the computer remembers. set erases and writes a number. change adds to what's already there.
  2. Make the variable (2m). Variables (orange) → Make a Variable → score, keep For all sprites → OK. Point at the checkbox that shows it live on stage.
  3. The game plan (2m). Show the flowchart slide: apple starts at the top at a random x → falls → touching the cat? yes = +1 → back to top. Say it as a story before any block moves.
  4. Build the apple live (4m). Build it slowly and name each category as you use it (Motion, Control, Sensing, Operators, Variables). The bottom check is the green < block from Operators with the y position reporter (bottom of Motion) dropped into its left slot:
    when ⚑ clicked
    set score to 0
    forever
    go to x: pick random -200 to 200 y: 170
    repeat until touching Cat?
    change y by -8
    if y position < -170 then
    go to x: pick random -200 to 200 y: 170
    change score by 1
    Read it as a story: start at the top somewhere random, fall until the cat catches you, jump back to the top if you drop past the bottom, and when you're caught, add a point. Then do it all again, forever.
  5. Catcher preview (1m). Show the Cat script on the slide and let them build it themselves in the lab.
Lab — Catch the Apple

Circulate for: change score by 1 placed below the repeat-until's C-mouth (not inside it); touching dropdown set to Cat, not "mouse-pointer"; set score to 0 before the forever loop; mouse x (Sensing), not typed-in text; the bottom check is y position < -170, not touching edge?.

Teach-It role-play — Design first, and the scope trap (5 min)
Kid card · Theo, age 10

Confident, talks fast, has played a lot of games. Announces he wants to make "Minecraft but with dragons, and fifty levels, and online." Gets impatient with paper: "Can we just start?"

The coach walks Theo through the one-page design (Goal · Controls · Scoring · Lose condition) and steers him to one working mechanic, without saying "no."

Debrief: a finished tiny game beats an abandoned huge one, for the child's motivation and the parent's.

Watch-outs
  • This lesson feels hard, so say so. Four categories plus a variable is a real step up. Adults compare themselves to the fast finisher at the next table. Tell them the lesson's growth-mindset point: "Big projects feel hard for everyone. Get one piece working, then the next."
  • Nesting mistakes. Dropping a block into the wrong C-mouth is the #1 error. Teach them to watch for the white "landing shadow" before releasing the mouse.
  • Score jumps by many at once: the apple is still touching the cat for several frames. The go to the top at the start of the next loop fixes it. Check that it's there.
  • "Why not touching edge?" Someone will swap it in for the bottom check, because it sounds right. The apple starts at y: 170, where it is already touching the top edge, so it's sent back to the top every frame and never falls. y position < -170 means "near the bottom" and nothing else. Don't argue it now. Park it, because it's your live debugging demo in 3.3.
  • Score never goes up: the touching block names the wrong sprite, or the apple never reaches the cat (check the cat's y is −140 and the apple's bottom check is −170, below it).
  • Screen-time guilt sometimes surfaces here ("my kid already plays too many games"). Reframe: making a game is creating, not consuming. Then move on. Don't let it take over the lab.
  • Unsaved work. This game is the basis for the Bug Hunt and Module 4. Make everyone save before the role-play.
Check — quiz

Answers: a variable is a named box that stores a value the program can change; add a point with change score by 1; set score to 0 goes right after the green flag, before the game loop; to see the score on the stage, tick the checkbox next to the variable.

3.3

Debugging & Remixing

30 min
Objectives
  • Apply the 4-step method: Watch → Predict → Change ONE thing → Test
  • Recognize the usual-suspect Scratch bugs and their fixes
  • Use See Inside on a shared project; explain remixing and its etiquette
  • Coach a frustrated child through a bug without taking over the mouse
Teaching sequence
  1. The mindset (2m).
    The computer isn't wrong and it isn't being mean. It's doing exactly what the blocks say. Debugging is the detective game of finding the gap between what you said and what you meant.
    Swap "It's broken, I failed" for "Interesting, let's find out why."
  2. The 4-step loop (3m). Show the slide. Stress step 3: change only one thing. Then name Scratch's helpers: click a single block to run it, tick a variable's checkbox to watch it, add a temporary say (score), slow things down with wait. Demo the rubber duck: explain a script to the stuffed animal and "find" a bug mid-sentence.
  3. Live bug: "Why not touching edge?" (included in the 3m above, or take 1m from See Inside). In your 3.2 game, swap the bottom check for if touching edge? and run it, thinking aloud through the four steps. Watch: the apple is frozen at the top. Predict: "It's being sent back to the top, so the if must be true right away. Is the apple touching an edge at y: 170?" (Yes, the top one.) Change one thing: put y position < -170 back. Test: it falls.
    The block did exactly what I told it: "touching any edge." I meant "the bottom." That gap between what I said and what I meant is the bug.
  4. Usual suspects + See Inside (3m). Walk the table on the slide quickly (it's in the workbook). Open any shared project on scratch.mit.edu → See Inside. Mention that Remix needs a signed-in account and that Scratch credits the original creator automatically.
  5. Bug Hunt launch. Form debugging-buddy pairs (below) and hand out 4-step cards. Say there are four bugs, but not what they are.
Lab — The Bug Hunt (17 min)

4.1

Clones — One Sprite, Many Copies

40 min · S4
Objectives
  • Explain a clone with the rubber-stamp idea (original = stamp, clones = prints)
  • Use create clone of myself, when I start as a clone, and delete this clone
  • Build the "hidden original stamps clones in a forever loop" pattern
  • Know what a clone copies (position, costume, size, visibility) and why clones must be deleted (limit ≈ 300)
Teaching sequence
  1. Why one sprite isn't enough (3m). "In 3.2 one apple fell. What if we wanted twenty?" Duplicating 20 sprites means editing 20 times. Clones: write the code once, make copies while the program runs.
  2. The stamp (4m). Hold up a real rubber stamp (or cookie cutter). Press three prints. Demo script: "The stamp stays in my hand. The prints are on the paper. In Scratch the original sprite is the stamp and it usually hides; the clones are the prints."
  3. The three blocks, live (5m). Show them at the very bottom of Control (adults scroll past them). Build the two-script shape: original hide → forever → create clone of myself → wait 1 seconds; clone when I start as a clone → show → say "I'm a copy!" for 2 seconds → delete this clone. Delete the wait briefly to show the flood, then put it back.
  4. The four rules (3m). Clones copy everything, including being hidden (so clones need show); each clone runs its own copy of the clone script; when ⚑ clicked runs only on the original; always clean up (about 300 clones max; the stop sign clears them all). Ask the room: "Is this code running on the stamp, the prints, or both?"
  5. Lab launch (from 15m). Falling stars. Warn them up front: "When you click the flag after the first script, nothing happens. That's correct."
Lab — Falling Stars (17 min)

New project; add the Star sprite and a dark backdrop (Stars or Night City). Original: hide, then forever go to x: (pick random -220 to 220) y: 180 → create clone of myself → wait 1 seconds. Clone: show → repeat until ⟨(y position) < -170⟩ → change y by -5, then delete this clone. Early finishers: random size and color, spinning stars, or snow. Reference: labs/module-4/l4-1-falling-stars.sb3. Everyone saves it: it feeds the capstone.

Circulate for: show missing from the clone script (nothing appears); the falling code under the green flag (the original falls); no go to before stamping (stars clump in one spot); y position dropped in the wrong slot of the < block; until touching edge used instead (each star deletes itself instantly because it's born touching the top).

Teach-It role-play — "Stamps and prints" (6 min)

Pairs: one adult takes the kid card, the other coaches. Coach's goal: let the child discover the invisible-clone bug instead of fixing it. Swap once at 3 minutes.

Kid card — Theo, age 9

Excited and fast. You've just built the stamp script and clicked the flag. Nothing appears, and you say "It's broken! Scratch is broken!" If your coach grabs the mouse, fold your arms. If they ask you a question about the stamp, think out loud and find it yourself. Later, try putting create clone of myself inside the clone script "to get more stars."

Where's the stamp right now? In my hand, not on the paper. So what should each print do after it lands?

We hid the stamp. The prints are copies of the stamp… so what do the prints look like right now?

Who should do the stamping — the stamp or the prints?

Debrief (1 line each): What did it feel like not to fix it? Which question worked?

Watch-outs
  • "I'm lost" fog. Clones are the first time lots happens at once, and adults who kept up easily in Module 3 may freeze. Name it: "Confusion here is your brain stretching. The stamp-and-prints question solves most of it."
  • Can't find the blocks. The clone blocks sit below the loops and ifs at the very bottom of Control; the hat block only peeks in. Show where to scroll.
  • Trackpad drags into the wrong slot. Dropping y position into the < block is fiddly. Mice help; so does zooming the code area (the + magnifier bottom-right).
  • Flood of clones. Someone will remove the wait or stamp from inside the clone script. The red stop sign clears all clones; reassure them nothing is broken.
  • Screen-time guilt surfaces when adults see how absorbing this is. Reframe: making a game is creating, not consuming.
Check — quiz

Answers: clones don't appear because they start hidden like the original and need show (B); the script that runs only on clones is when I start as a clone (C); slowing then stopping = clones never deleted, piling toward the 300 limit (A); create clone inside the clone script = every clone makes another, flooding the screen until the limit (B).

  • Lives never reach exactly 0 if reset game doesn't set lives to 3, or lives were made "For this sprite only" on the Bowl (the Star then can't see it).
  • Publishing pressure. Kids will ask to put it online. "Not tonight. Next session we learn how to share safely (Lesson 5.2)."
  • Showcase shyness. Some children won't speak to strangers. Let them point and press the flag while the parent reads the design card aloud.
Check — quiz

Answers: lives drop by 2–3 because delete this clone isn't inside the lives if (B); the Game Over sprite shows first so the sign is on screen when everything freezes (A); game breaks near the finish → shrink it to the last change, or save and stop (B); GAME OVER still visible at the start of a new game → add when ⚑ clicked → hide to the Game Over sprite (C).

Across two sessions the grown-ups learned clones and custom blocks, then used them with their child from a paper plan to launch day. Recap: stamp and prints → recipe cards → design card → smallest playable version → one feature at a time → launch. Preview S6: "Tonight you probably noticed how hard it is to keep your hands off the mouse. Next session is all about that: teaching Scratch to your child."

Lesson Plans

5.1

The Art of Not Taking Over

20 min
Objectives
  • Use scaffolding: the least help that unblocks, then fade it (I lead → they lead, I prompt → they do it, I watch → they teach)
  • Ask guiding questions instead of giving answers
  • Praise effort and strategy, not talent; use "yet"
  • Keep a child in productive struggle: raise the bar when bored, shrink the step when frustrated, break when boiling over
Teaching sequence
  1. Hook from the warm-up (2m). "Hands up if you grabbed the mouse at Family Build Night." Raise your own hand first. "Every time we fix it for them, we quietly teach 'this is too hard for you.'"
  2. The scaffold ladder (2m). Draw the four rungs on the board. Then the three-column contrast: "Figure it out" (walks away) · "Which category might have a move block?" · "Drag the blue block here." Ask the room which one is the scaffold.
  3. Live-demo script: ask, don't tell (2m). Open any buggy project (the labs/module-3/l3-3-bug-hunt-starter.sb3 file is ideal). Have a volunteer drive. You sit on your hands, visibly, and use only the five toolkit questions: "What do you want to happen? What happened instead? Which block is in charge of that? What could we try to test it? Where have we seen this before?" Count to five out loud the first time you pause.
  4. Process praise + "yet" (2m). Read the "say this / go easy on this" pairs from the slide. One line: "Praise what they did, because they can do it again." Model a "yet" sentence.
  5. Role-play launch (10m + 2m debrief). See Lab and Teach-It below.
Lab — Hands-Off Coaching Rounds

Pairs, one laptop, one buggy project (labs/module-3/l3-3-bug-hunt-starter.sb3, or any participant's half-finished Star Catcher). The "child" holds the mouse; the coach may not touch it. Two 4-minute rounds, swapping roles and drawing a new kid card each time. Cards: labs/module-5/role-play-cards.html (kid cards K1–K6).

Circulate for: coaches whose hands drift to the mouse or trackpad; questions that are really answers ("Don't you think it should be mouse x?"); "you're so smart"; coaches who never shrink the step when the "child" escalates.

Teach-It role-play

Kid card K1 (demo it yourself first): Maya, 9. Frustrated. Her Star Catcher stars fall but the score never goes up. After one minute she pushes the laptop toward you: "Just do it for me."

What do you want to happen when a star hits the bowl? … And what happens instead?
Let's read the star's script out loud together. When does it check for the bowl?
How about I'm your hands and you're the boss? I'll only drag the blocks you point to.
You tested one block at a time until you found it. That's exactly what real coders do.
You can't make it score… yet. Let's shrink it: just get one star to say "caught!" first.

Debrief question: "Coaches, what was hardest? 'Children', which sentence made you want to keep going?"

Watch-outs
  • Guilt spiral. Someone will say "I've said 'you're so smart' a thousand times." Answer from the lesson: warmth matters more than perfect wording; just add process praise alongside it. The shift happens over months.
  • "Isn't it mean to watch them struggle?" Struggling alone would be. Sitting beside them, asking questions and shrinking the step is support that trusts them.
  • Over-acting. Adults playing a child can go full tantrum. Keep it realistic; the card's behavior is the brief, not a stage direction.
  • Trackpad reflex. On a laptop the coach's hand is inches from the trackpad. Have coaches literally sit on their hands or hold a cup.
  • Phones. This is the session where family logistics buzz loudest (pick-up for the showcase). Offer a 30-second "text your family the arrival time" moment in the warm-up so phones can go face-down.
Check — quiz

Answers: scaffolding = just enough help, then fade it; best resilience praise = "You kept trying different things until it worked"; bored child → offer a new challenge ("What if it kept score?"); child says "just do it for me" → be their hands while they're the boss.

5.2

Sharing Safely & the Scratch Community

18 min
Objectives
  • Explain accounts, the parent email, and why Scratch collects minimal data (non-profit, COPPA-compliant)
  • Show that projects are private until the child clicks Share, and that an account is optional
  • Name the community protections: moderation, guidelines, Report, comments off, delete, no private messaging
  • Draft family sharing rules and hold the "before sharing" conversation
Teaching sequence
  1. Name the worry (1m). "This is the part parents worry about most. Let's look at exactly how it works." Worry is a sign of paying attention, not paranoia.
  2. Live-demo script: private by default (5m). Logged in on the projector, open My Stuff. Point to an unshared project: "Nobody can see this." Open it, point at the orange Share button: "Nothing goes public until your child clicks this." Then open a shared project page and show the comment area, the Report button, and the "Comments on/off" toggle. Say plainly: "There are no private messages on Scratch. Everything is public and visible to you." Finish on scratch.mit.edu/parents as the official source.
  3. Safety checklist + family rules (7m). Hand out labs/module-5/safety-checklist.html. Participants tick what they've already done and draft three or four family rules in the box: what never gets shared, where Scratch is used, what to do with a mean comment.
  4. Scenario role-play (5m). See Teach-It below.
Lab — Safety Checklist & Family Sharing Rules

Each family completes labs/module-5/safety-checklist.html and drafts their sharing rules to sign with their child at home. Then pairs draw one scenario card (S1–S8) from labs/module-5/role-play-cards.html.

Circulate for: usernames that include a real name or birth year; rules phrased only as "don'ts" (nudge toward "if anyone's unkind, show me and we'll handle it together"); parents who assume sharing is required (it isn't).

Teach-It role-play

Kid card K7: Leo, 11. Excited. Wants to share Star Catcher right now so his friends can play it. He's picked the username "LeoMartinez2014".

I love that you want people to play it. Before we share, let's pick a username that doesn't say who you are. Something about stars or cats?
When we share, kids around the world can see it and comment. Most are super nice! Our family rules: no real name, where we live, or our school, and if anyone's ever unkind, you show me and we handle it together. Deal?
Your game started from the course, so let's add a thank-you in the Notes and Credits. Crediting others is what good Scratchers do.
Watch-outs
  • Home tech-support questions ("How do I lock down my son's Chromebook?"). Park them on a "Parking lot" sheet; answer after class or point to the device maker. Stay on Scratch.
  • Screen-time guilt. Reframe: Scratch time is making time. The healthy-habits list says nudge "let's make" over "let's watch" — that's the lever, not a total ban.
  • Stale specifics. Scratch's policies and features change. Don't improvise details you're unsure of: send people to the official Parents, Community Guidelines and Privacy Policy pages.
  • Over-promising. Moderation is strong but not perfect. Say so; the family rule for mean comments is the real safety net.
Check — quiz

Answers: public only after they click Share; the community is moderated, has guidelines, and has no private DMs; best young-sharer habit = non-identifying username, your email on the account, regular check-ins; unkind comment → read it together, report it, delete it, talk about how it felt.

5.3

ScratchJr — Coding for Ages 5–7

17 min
Objectives
  • Explain how ScratchJr differs from Scratch: picture blocks in a left-to-right row, grid squares, tablets, no online community
  • Recognize the six colors: yellow Triggering, blue Motion, purple Looks, green Sound, orange Control, red End
  • Build the tap-to-talk pet ("My Pet Says Hi")
  • Coach a 4–7-year-old: 10–15-minute sessions, story first, you read / they tap
Teaching sequence
  1. Differences in two minutes (2m). Same big ideas (events start scripts, blocks run in order, loops repeat); different everything else. Point out the one that relaxes parents: projects stay on the device, no comments or strangers.
  2. The color trap (1m). In Scratch green = Operators and sound is pink. In ScratchJr green = Sound and yellow starts things. "Trust the pictures."
  3. Live-demo script (2m). Mirror a tablet if you can (or hold it under a document camera). Tap +, pick an animal. Yellow → drag Start on Tap. Blue → snap Hop to the right. Purple → Grow. Green → microphone, record "Hi! I'm your pet!". Purple → Shrink. Red → End. Tap the pet. Then say: "Without Shrink, it gets bigger with every tap."
  4. Build it (7m). In twos or threes per tablet. Fast finishers add page 2 with Go to Page.
  5. 5-year-old role-play (5m). See Teach-It below.
Lab — "My Pet Says Hi"

Build the row Start on Tap → Hop 2 → Grow 2 → My Sound → Shrink 2 → End on a tablet. No Scratch file: ScratchJr projects live on the device. No tablets? Use the cut-out paper ScratchJr blocks at the end of labs/module-5/role-play-cards.html: participants lay out the row on the table left to right and "run" it by acting it out.

Circulate for: blocks not snapped into one row (nothing happens on tap); Green Flag used instead of Start on Tap; Grow with no Shrink; silent recordings (microphone permission off in the tablet's settings).

Teach-It role-play

Kid card K8: Ava, 5. Can't read yet. Wiggly. Wants to record a burp for the puppy and keeps dragging blocks back onto the palette (which deletes them). The coach's hands stay in their lap; they may steady the tablet.

Tell me a tiny story first. Who's in it, and what happens?
So the puppy jumps, gets really big, and barks. Which picture looks like jumping? … Yes, the hop! What comes next?
Whoa, that's not what we planned! What should we try?
You made the puppy do that! You told it what to do, and it listened. That's what coders do.
Watch-outs
  • "We don't have a tablet." ScratchJr is for tablets and Chromebooks. On a regular computer, use full Scratch with the Ages 5–7 tips: you read, they choose and press the green flag.
  • Adult fingers on a tablet. Drag-and-drop on glass is as fiddly for some adults as the trackpad was in S1. Offer a stylus if you have one; reassure that a block dragged back to the palette is simply deleted, no harm done.
  • Color confusion for the facilitator too: rehearse the six colors before class.
  • "Is 4 too young?" Not with a lap to sit on and very short sessions. If it isn't fun yet, try again in a few months.
Check — quiz

Answers: ScratchJr blocks sit in a horizontal row, read left to right; react to a touch = Start on Tap; session length for a 5-year-old = about 10–15 minutes, ending while it's still fun; pet grows with every tap → add Shrink (same number) or Reset Size.

5.4

What Comes After Scratch

10 min
Objectives
  • Place ScratchJr, Scratch, physical computing and text languages on one road map
  • Name what's still ahead inside Scratch: lists (Variables → Make a List) and extensions (Pen, Music, Text to Speech, Video Sensing, micro:bit)
  • Explain why the jump to Python is mostly syntax: the concepts transfer
  • Leave with trusted next resources and one "next project" per family
Teaching sequence
  1. The road map (2m). ScratchJr (5–7) → Scratch (8+) → three branches: deeper Scratch, physical computing (Makey Makey 7+, LEGO robotics 8+, micro:bit 9+), text languages (Python, JavaScript). "There's no single correct path."
  2. Live-demo script: the bridge (3m). Put the Scratch stack repeat 3 → say Hi! beside for i in range(3): print("Hi!"). Ask: "What's the same?" Let the room answer: the loop, the count, the message. "Your child already owns the concepts. Python just types them." Then click Add Extension (bottom-left of the editor) to show Pen and Music exist.
  3. Next-project plan (5m). In the workbook, each participant names the next project their child is excited about and which road-map branch it leads to. Two or three share aloud.
Lab — My Family's Next Project

Workbook page "What Next" (take-home handout). No Scratch file. Fast finishers draw a scenario card (S7–S8 fit 5.4) and rehearse with a partner.

Circulate for: plans that are the parent's idea ("She should learn Python") rather than the child's; nudge back to "What does your child light up about?"

Teach-It role-play

Kid card K10: Sam, 12. Bored and a bit dismissive: "Scratch is for babies. I want to do real coding."

You already know real coding. Look: this Scratch loop and this Python loop are the same idea, just typed.
What would you want to make if you could make anything? Let's find the tool that fits it.
Look how far you've come. Remember when we made the cat just say hello? What do you want to make next?
Watch-outs
  • Parent anxiety about "falling behind." Many kids happily stay in Scratch into their early teens. Switch when curious, not on a schedule (typing fluency and reading comfort help; many start around 10–13).
  • "My child is already better than me." Your job changes, it doesn't end: audience, question-asker, cheerleader.
  • Buying hardware on impulse. Suggest trying the free options first (offline app, Pen/Music extensions, Code.org, the library coding club).
Check — quiz

Answers: natural next step inside Scratch = lists, or an extension like Pen or Music; Scratch → Python isn't scary because the core concepts transfer; only the syntax changes; best momentum = follow their interests with another exciting project; extensions live behind the Add Extension button, bottom-left of the editor.

S6

Wrap-Up, Showcase & Graduation

10 + 25 min
Wrap-up before the children arrive (10m)
  1. Final journal (6m). "Flip back to your first entry from Lesson 1.1. Read your why, your worry and your confidence number. Now write today's number." Offer, never require, one share.
  2. Hand out "What Next" (2m). The last page of the workbook: road map, the trusted resources from 5.4, the family's next project.
  3. Evaluation (2m). Hand out your course evaluation while children arrive.
Showcase & graduation (25m)
  1. Welcome the children (3m). "Tonight the coders present and the grown-ups cheer." Remind families: photos only with a yes on the consent sheet.
  2. Showcase (14m). Up to ~10 families: 60–90 seconds each on the projector. The child presents their Star Catcher (or any project) and shows one thing they're proud of; the parent adds one sentence: "A moment I kept my hands off the mouse was…". More than 10 families: run a gallery walk instead, laptops open around the room, with sticky notes for kind, curious, specific comments (lesson 5.2's gold standard: "I love how… — how did you do that?").
  3. Graduation (6m). Read each name, hand over the certificate. Nice touch: the child hands the certificate to their parent.
  4. Close (2m). One line: "You started this course unsure whether you were 'techy enough.' You finished it having built a game and shown your child that grown-ups learn hard things too." Point to the library or school coding club if one exists.
Certificate text (suggested)

Certificate of Completion — Scratch for Parents: Learn It, Then Teach It. This certifies that __________ completed six workshop sessions (17 lessons): writing their first programs, building a complete game with clones and custom blocks, and learning the craft of coaching a young coder. Awarded on __________ · Facilitator __________.

Optional child certificate — "Official Scratch Coder": __________ designed and built a game, fixed real bugs, and taught a grown-up something new. Keep making wonderful things!

Watch-outs
  • A project that won't load (no Wi-Fi, forgotten password). Have the display laptop signed out and ready for .sb3 files from USB; failing that, the child describes it and demos next time. Never let a family end on a failed load: celebrate the attempt.
  • Shy presenters. Offer "you drive, your parent talks" or a presenting pair of siblings. Nobody is made to speak.
  • Comparisons. Some projects will be far more elaborate. Praise process in every introduction ("…who kept testing until the stars fell").
