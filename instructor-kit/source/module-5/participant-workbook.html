5.1

The Art of Not Taking Over

20 min
By the end you can
  • Give the least help that unblocks your child, then fade it
  • Ask a guiding question instead of giving the answer
  • Praise effort and strategy, and teach the word "yet"
Key ideas
  • The struggle is the lesson. Fixing it for them quietly says "this is too hard for you."
  • Scaffolding is help that fades: I lead → they lead, I prompt → they do it, I watch → they teach me or a sibling.
  • Question toolkit: What do you want to happen? What happened instead? Which block is in charge of that? What could we try to test it? Where have we seen this before?
  • Praise the process ("You tested one block at a time"), not the talent ("You're a genius"). Add "…yet" to every "I can't."
  • Productive struggle: bored → raise the bar; frustrated → shrink the step; boiling over → take a break (a real strategy, not a failure).
Worked example — coaching a real bug

Your child's Star Catcher stars fall, but the score never goes up. Here's the star clone's script:

when I start as a clone
go to x: pick random -220 to 220 y: 170
show
if touching Bowl? then
change score by 1
delete this clone
forever
change y by -5

You see it instantly: the if touching Bowl? check runs once, at the top of the screen, before the star falls. It belongs inside the forever. Don't say that. Climb the help ladder one rung at a time and stop as soon as your child is moving again:

  1. "What do you want to happen when a star hits the bowl?"
  2. "Which script is in charge of the score?"
  3. "Let's read the star's script out loud. When does it check for the bowl?"
  4. "What would happen if the check were inside the loop?"
  5. Last resort: point to the if block and the forever block. They still do the dragging.
Your turn — Hands-off coaching rounds
  1. Pair up. The "child" draws a kid card and holds the mouse. The coach may not touch the mouse or trackpad.
  2. Open the buggy project your facilitator provides. Coach for 4 minutes using only questions and process praise.
  3. Swap roles, draw a new card, repeat.
Teach-It practice
What do you want to happen? … And what happened instead?
How about I'm your hands and you're the boss? I'll only drag the blocks you point to.
You kept trying different things until it worked.
You can't make the stars fall… yet. Let's just get one to move first.

5.2

Sharing Safely & the Scratch Community

18 min
By the end you can
  • Set up a Scratch account the safe way (or decide not to need one yet)
  • Explain "private until shared" and the community's protections
  • Write family sharing rules and have the "before sharing" talk
Key ideas
  • Scratch is run by a non-profit, collects minimal data, and asks for a parent/guardian email.
  • Projects are private until your child clicks Share. Sharing is a choice, and an account is optional (File → Save to your computer works without one).
  • The community is moderated, has short kid-readable Community Guidelines, and has no private messaging.
  • Comment controls: Report, turn comments off, delete comments, report and block unwanted users.
  • It's still the internet: shared device or common room, regular check-ins, and "show me any mean comment."
Worked example — one family's sharing rules
The Rivera family Scratch rules

1. Our username never has a real name, school, or birth year. (StarChef_77 ✓ · LeoMartinez2014 ✗)

2. No real name, address, school, phone number, or photos of us in projects or comments.

3. Scratch lives on the family laptop in the kitchen.

4. If anyone is ever unkind, we show a grown-up and handle it together: read it, report it, delete it.

5. When we remix, we say thank you in the Notes and Credits.

Signed: Leo ______   Mom ______

Your turn — Safety checklist & family rules
  1. Tick through the one-page Scratch Safety Checklist your facilitator hands out.
  2. Draft three or four rules for your family below. At home, rewrite them with your child and both sign.
Teach-It practice
When we share your project, other kids around the world can see it and leave comments. Most are super nice! Our family rules: we never share private stuff, and if anyone's ever unkind, you show me and we handle it together. Deal?
Let's write a comment together: kind, curious, specific. "I love how the dragon breathes fire — how did you do that?"
Not everyone will love everything we make, and that's okay. We learn from helpful notes and ignore unkind ones.

5.3

ScratchJr — Coding for Ages 5–7

17 min
By the end you can
  • Explain how ScratchJr differs from Scratch
  • Build a tap-to-talk pet with a recorded sound
  • Run a short, playful session with a 4–7-year-old
Key ideas
  • ScratchJr is a free tablet app (iPad, Android, Chromebook) with picture blocks, so no reading is needed.
  • Blocks snap into a row read left to right, like a sentence. Motion numbers count grid squares.
  • Six colors: yellow Triggering · blue Motion · purple Looks · green Sound · orange Control · red End. (Not the same as Scratch: trust the pictures.)
  • Up to four pages per project, turned with Go to Page. Projects stay on the device: no online community.
  • Coach in 10–15-minute sessions: story first, you read, they tap, lots of silly sounds.
Worked example — "My Pet Says Hi"
👆 Start on Tap ⤴️ Hop 2 🔼 Grow 2 🎙️ My Sound 🔽 Shrink 2 ⏹️ End

Tap the pet: it hops, puffs up, says hi in a real voice, and settles back down. Shrink matters: without it the pet grows bigger with every tap. It's the same thinking as your first Scratch cat (an event, a sequence, a sound), laid out sideways. A rough Scratch equivalent stacks top to bottom (a "hop" becomes up, then back down):

when this sprite clicked
change y by 20
change size by 20
play sound recording1 until done
change size by -20
change y by -20
Your turn — Build it on a tablet
  1. Open ScratchJr → house icon → big +. Tap + beside the characters and pick an animal.
  2. Yellow: drag Start on Tap. Blue: snap Hop on its right. Purple: Grow. Tap the pet to test.
  3. Green: tap the microphone, record "Hi! I'm your pet!", accept, and snap the sound on.
  4. Purple: Shrink. Red: End. Tap the pet.
  5. Stretch: add page 2 (+ in the page strip) and end page 1's green-flag script with Go to Page.

No tablet? Plan a tiny two-page story your child could tell, one block per box:

Teach-It practice
Tell me a tiny story first. Who's in it, and what happens?
Which picture looks like jumping? … Yes, the hop! What comes next?
Whoa, that's not what we planned! What should we try?
You made the puppy do that! You told it what to do, and it listened. That's what coders do.

5.4

What Comes After Scratch

10 min
By the end you can
  • Place ScratchJr, Scratch, hardware and text code on one road map
  • Name what's still ahead inside Scratch (lists, extensions)
  • Explain why the jump to Python is mostly syntax
Key ideas
  • Road map: ScratchJr (5–7) → Scratch (8+) → deeper Scratch, physical computing, or text languages. There's no single correct path.
  • Still ahead in Scratch: lists (Variables → Make a List) and extensions via the Add Extension button, bottom-left: Pen, Music, Text to Speech, Video Sensing, micro:bit.
  • Beyond the screen: Makey Makey (7+), LEGO robotics (8+), micro:bit (9+).
  • The concepts transfer. Sequences, loops, conditionals, variables and events exist in every language.
  • Momentum comes from interest. One more exciting project beats rushing to the next tool.
Worked example — the same loop, two ways
repeat 3
say Hi! for 1 seconds
for i in range(3):
    print("Hi!")

Same loop, same count, same message. Your child already owns the idea; Python just types it.
