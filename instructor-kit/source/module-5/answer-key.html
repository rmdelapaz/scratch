5.1 · The Art of Not Taking Over

Q1
What is "scaffolding" in teaching?
A) Giving just enough help, then fading it as they gain confidence
Why: like training wheels raised bit by bit. B turns the child into a spectator; C leaves them stalled.
Q2
Which praise best builds resilience?
B) "You kept trying different things until it worked."
Why: it names a strategy the child can repeat. "You're so smart" makes the next bug feel like proof of the opposite; "that was easy" dismisses their effort.
Q3
Your child is bored because the task is too easy. Best response?
A) Offer a new challenge ("What if it kept score?")
Why: bored = raise the bar back into productive struggle. Simplifying (B) makes boredom worse.
Q4
"Just do it for me!" Best response?
B) Offer to be their "hands" while they're the "boss"
Why: the lesson's own answer. The child keeps all the thinking; within a session or two most kids want the mouse back. A robs the learning moment; C is "too little help."
Lab — Hands-off coaching rounds: what good looks like

The prop is labs/module-3/l3-3-bug-hunt-starter.sb3 (four planted bugs; the fixed version is l3-2-catch-the-apple.sb3) or any half-finished Star Catcher. Participants don't need to fix every bug: the goal is the coaching, not the project.

Look-fors: coach's hands never touch the mouse or trackpad · at least three toolkit questions · a visible pause before speaking · at least one process-praise line · a "yet" · coach shrinks the step when the "child" escalates · the "child" does every drag.

Worked-example bug (workbook) — the fix

The star's if touching Bowl? check sat above the forever, so it ran once, at the top of the screen. It must live inside the loop:

when I start as a clone
go to x: pick random -220 to 220 y: 170
show
forever
change y by -5
if touching Bowl? then
change score by 1
delete this clone
Common wrong moves & how to coach them

The disguised answer ("Don't you think it should go inside the forever?") → "That's the answer with a question mark. Try: when does it check?"

The walk-away ("Figure it out") → too little help; move one rung down the ladder, don't leave.

Talent praise on success ("You're a genius!") → swap for what they did: "You read the script line by line."

Never shrinking the step while the "child" gets louder → "What's the tiniest thing that would be a win right now?"

5.2 · Sharing Safely & the Scratch Community

Q1
When is a child's Scratch project visible to the public?
B) Only after they click "Share"
Why: projects are private by default; saving (C) keeps them in the account, not public.
Q2
Which is TRUE about the Scratch community?
A) It's moderated, has guidelines, and has no private DMs
Why: interactions are public and visible to you, and every comment can be reported, deleted, or turned off.
Q3
Best safety habit for a young sharer?
B) A non-identifying username, your email on the account, and regular check-ins
Why: hides real identity, keeps you in the loop on account notices, and makes "show me what you made" a normal habit.
Q4
Your child gets an unkind comment. Best first response?
C) Read it together, report it, delete it, and talk about how it felt
Why: the lesson's "team job." Consider turning comments off on that project for a while. Deleting the account (A) punishes the child; replying (B) feeds the conflict.
Lab — Safety checklist & family rules: what good looks like

A finished labs/module-5/safety-checklist.html plus 3–5 family rules that cover: what never gets shared (real name, address, school, phone, photos) · where Scratch is used (shared device / common room) · what to do with a mean comment (show a grown-up; read, report, delete together) · ideally credit when remixing. Rules are written with the child at home and signed by both.

Scenario role-play look-fors: the coach says yes to the child's excitement before setting guard-rails · proposes a non-identifying username · uses the "before sharing" script or their own version of it · treats remixing as good, with credit.

Common wrong answers & how to coach them

"Kids have to share to use Scratch." No: an account is optional, and projects can stay private forever.

Username like "EmmaJones2015". Real name + birth year. Suggest interest-based names (StarChef_77).

Rules that are only "don'ts." Add the positive rule: "If anyone's unkind, show me and we'll handle it together." That's the one that keeps kids talking to you.

5.3 · ScratchJr — Coding for Ages 5–7

Q1
How are blocks arranged in a ScratchJr script?
B) In a horizontal row, read left to right
Why: like a sentence. Scratch stacks top to bottom (A); order always matters (C).
Q2
React when your child touches the pet on screen?
C) Start on Tap
Why: Start on Bump fires when two characters touch; Green Flag starts when the flag is pressed.
Q3
Best session length for a 5-year-old?
A) About 10–15 minutes, ending while it's still fun
Why: stop after a big laugh and they'll ask to do it again tomorrow. Persistence isn't learned by pushing a five-year-old to tears.
Q4
The pet ends up bigger after every tap. Fix?
A) Add a Shrink block (same number) or Reset Size before End
Why: Grow changes size permanently; the script must undo it. From the lesson's common-problems table.
Lab — "My Pet Says Hi": the reference row
👆 Start on Tap ⤴️ Hop 2 🔼 Grow 2 🎙️ My Sound 🔽 Shrink 2 ⏹️ End

Stretch (two-page story): page 1 — Start on Green Flag → Say "Come to my house!" → Wait 10 → Go to Page 2; page 2 — Start on Green Flag → Move Right 3 → Say "Welcome home!" → End. Go to Page only appears in the red End category once a second page exists.

Role-play look-fors: story asked for before any block · coach reads, "child" taps · hands in lap (steadying the tablet is fine) · a laugh at the unexpected ("Whoa, that's not what we planned!") · ends on a win.

Common problems & how to coach them

Tapping does nothing → script starts with Green Flag, or a block isn't snapped on. "Drag them until they click into one row."

Pet walks off the stage → moves add up each run; start the row with blue Go Home.

Silent recording → allow the microphone for ScratchJr in the tablet's settings.

A block "vanished" → it was dragged back onto the palette, which deletes it. Drag a fresh one out.

Color mix-ups ("green is Operators!") → that's Scratch. In ScratchJr green is Sound. Trust the pictures.

5.4 · What Comes After Scratch

Q1
Natural next thing to explore inside Scratch after Star Catcher?
B) Lists, or an extension like Pen or Music
Why: Scratch has years of depth; there's no rush to leave it (C).
Q2
Why is Scratch → Python not as scary as it sounds?
A) The core concepts transfer; only the syntax changes
Why: sequences, loops, conditionals, variables and events exist in every language. They're not identical (B), and nobody starts from zero (C).
Q3
Best way to keep a child's coding momentum?
B) Follow their interests with another project they're excited about
Why: interest sustains itself; pressure (A, C) burns it out.
Q4
Where are the Pen and Music blocks?
B) Behind the Add Extension button, bottom-left of the editor
Why: extensions add whole new block categories (Pen, Music, Text to Speech, Video Sensing, micro:bit). Scratch is free: there is no paid version (C).
Lab — Our next project: what good looks like

A named project the child is excited about (not the parent's plan), tied to one road-map branch. Examples: a high-score list for Star Catcher (deeper Scratch: lists) · a drawing toy (Pen extension) · a banana piano (Makey Makey) · a first Python loop (text code).

repeat 3
say Hi! for 1 seconds
for i in range(3):
    print("Hi!")
Common wrong answers & how to coach them

"They should start Python at 8 so they don't fall behind." Switch when curious, not on a schedule. Many kids start around 10–13; plenty stay in Scratch longer. Nothing is wasted.

"I can't keep up, so I'm done." Your job changes, it doesn't end: audience, question-asker, cheerleader.

S6 · Showcase & Graduation

What a good showcase looks like

The child presents and drives; the parent adds one sentence about a hands-off moment · every introduction praises process ("…who kept testing until the stars fell") · gallery-walk comments are kind, curious and specific · no photos without consent · every family leaves with a certificate, the "What Next" page, and one named next project.
