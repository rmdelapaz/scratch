🧸 Lesson 5.3: ScratchJr — Coding for Ages 5–7

Got a little one who wants to do what the big kids are doing, but can't read the blocks yet? ScratchJr is Scratch's younger sibling: picture blocks, big buttons, and no reading needed. In this lesson you'll learn it in minutes and discover how to coach a five-year-old coder.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain what ScratchJr is and how it differs from Scratch
  • Recognize the six color-coded block categories and read a script left to right
  • Build a tap-to-talk character with a recorded sound, then a two-page story
  • Run short, playful coding sessions that suit a 4–7-year-old's attention span
  • Decide when your child is ready to move up to full Scratch

🎬 Meet ScratchJr

ScratchJr is a free app made for children about 5 to 7 years old. It runs on iPads, Android tablets, and Chromebooks. Like Scratch, kids snap blocks together to make characters move, talk, and play sounds. The difference is that every block is a picture, so a child who can't read yet can still code.

By the end of this lesson you'll have built this: tap the pet, and it hops, grows, and says hello in your child's own recorded voice.

👆Start on Tap ⤴️Hop 2 🔼Grow 2 🎙️My Sound 🔽Shrink 2 ⏹️End

💡 Before you start

Install ScratchJr from your tablet's app store (search “ScratchJr”; it's free, with no ads or in-app purchases). Open it, tap the house icon, then tap the big + to start a new project. A cat appears on the stage, just like in Scratch.

🎓 Learn It: How ScratchJr differs from Scratch

Because you know Scratch, ScratchJr will feel familiar. The big ideas carry over: events start scripts, blocks run in order, loops repeat. What changes is everything that would trip up a small child.

ScratchScratchJr
Block directionBlocks stack top to bottomBlocks snap side by side in a row, read left to right like a sentence
ReadingEvery block has wordsBlocks show pictures; only a few, like “Say,” take typed words
Number of blocksWell over a hundredOnly a few dozen, so there's less to hunt through
DeviceComputer web browser (or the desktop app)Made for tablets: tap and drag with a finger
SharingOnline community, projects on the webProjects stay on the device, with no public community
Numbers“Steps” (tiny units)Grid squares, so a child can count moves on a visible grid

That last row about sharing is a quiet gift for parents. There are no comments, followers, or strangers to worry about. Projects live in the app on that tablet. Some versions let you send a copy by email or AirDrop, but there's no public website to post to. (The community safety habits from Lesson 5.2 can wait until your child moves up to Scratch.)

🎓 Learn It: Six colors of picture blocks

Tap a colored button on the left of the block area and that category's blocks slide into view. Color tells you what a block does, just like in Scratch, though some of the colors don't match the ones you already know:

  • 🟡 Triggering
  • 🔵 Motion
  • 🟣 Looks
  • 🟢 Sound
  • 🟠 Control
  • 🔴 End
ColorWhat it doesBlocks you'll use most
🟡 TriggeringStarts a script, like Scratch's EventsStart on Green Flag, Start on Tap (tapping the character), Start on Bump (touching another character), Start on Message
🔵 MotionMoves the character around the stageMove right/left/up/down, Turn, Hop, Go Home
🟣 LooksChanges how it looksSay (a speech bubble), Grow, Shrink, Reset Size, Hide, Show
🟢 SoundMakes noisePop, plus any sounds you record with the microphone
🟠 ControlChanges timing and repetitionWait, Stop, Set Speed, Repeat (wraps around other blocks)
🔴 EndCaps the end of a scriptEnd, Repeat Forever, Go to Page

Many blocks have a small number under them. For Motion blocks, that number counts grid squares. Tap the grid button above the stage to show the grid, and “Move right 3” becomes something a child can count with a finger: one, two, three squares.

🎨 Watch out: the colors don't match Scratch

In Scratch, green is Operators and sound is pink. In ScratchJr, green is Sound and yellow starts things. That doesn't bother kids at all, but it can confuse you if you're switching between the two apps. Trust the pictures.

🛠️ Try It: “My Pet Says Hi”

Build this yourself first (it takes about ten minutes) so you know where everything is before you sit down with your child.

  1. Pick or make a pet

    Tap the + beside the character list to open the library and choose an animal. Or tap the paintbrush to open the paint editor and draw one. The paint editor also has a camera tool: tap it, tap inside a shape, and take a photo, and the photo fills that shape. Kids love putting their own face on a character.

  2. Start on tap

    Tap the yellow button and drag Start on Tap (a finger touching a character) into the script area. Now the script will run whenever someone taps the pet.

  3. Hop and grow

    From blue, drag Hop so it snaps onto the right side of the trigger. From purple, snap Grow on after it. Tap the pet on the stage to test it.

    👆Start on Tap ⤴️Hop 2 🔼Grow 2

  4. Record a sound

    Tap green, then the microphone block. Press record, say “Hi! I'm your pet!” (or bark, or giggle), stop, and accept it. A new sound block appears. Snap it on after Grow.

  5. Shrink back and end

    Add a purple Shrink so the pet returns to normal size (otherwise it keeps growing with every tap), then cap the row with the red End block.

    👆Start on Tap ⤴️Hop 2 🔼Grow 2 🎙️My Sound 🔽Shrink 2 ⏹️End

✅ Tap the pet!

It hops, puffs up, says hi in a real voice, and settles back down. That's a complete program with an event, a sequence, and a sound. It's the same thinking as your first Scratch cat, laid out sideways.

🛠️ Try It: A two-page story

ScratchJr projects can have up to four pages, like pages in a picture book. Each page has its own background and characters. Let's make the pet invite us somewhere new.

  1. Add page 2

    Tap the + in the page strip on the right side of the screen. A blank page 2 appears. Tap the background button to pick a scene (a park, a bedroom, outer space) and add a character.

  2. On page 1, turn the page

    Go back to page 1. Give the pet a second script that starts with the green flag, says something, then ends with Go to Page. Once a second page exists, the red End category shows a Go to Page block with a little picture of that page.

    🏁Start on Green Flag 💬Say Come to my house! ⏱️Wait 10 📄Go to Page 2

  3. On page 2, say hello

    On page 2, give its character a green-flag script too. When the story turns to a new page, that page's green-flag scripts start up.

    🏁Start on Green Flag ➡️Move Right 3 💬Say Welcome home! ⏹️End

Press the green flag above the stage (or use the full-screen button to present it) and watch the story play across two pages. The pet from Try It 1 still responds to taps on page 1, too.

🐛 Common problems & fixes

What you seeLikely causeFix
Tapping the pet does nothingThe script starts with Green Flag, or a block isn't snapped onUse Start on Tap; drag blocks until they click together in one row
The pet gets bigger and biggerGrow with no Shrink or Reset SizeAdd Shrink (same number) or Reset Size at the end
The pet walks off the stageMoves add up each runAdd the blue Go Home block at the start of the row
Recording is silentThe app doesn't have microphone permissionAllow the microphone in the tablet's settings for ScratchJr
Page 2 never appearsNo Go to Page block, or the script before it never runsCheck page 1's row ends with Go to Page and starts with a trigger that actually fires
A block “vanished”It was dragged back onto the block palette, which deletes itDrag a fresh one out; no harm done

🌱 Growth mindset: wobbly is what learning looks like

Nobody rides a bike smoothly the first time. The wobbling is the learning. Your five-year-old will drop blocks in the wrong spot and make a pet that grows until it fills the screen, and you may find sideways rows odd after weeks of Scratch. That's experimenting, not failing. When a script flops, laugh and say, “Whoa, that's not what we planned! What should we try?” A child who hears that at five learns that surprises are interesting instead of scary.

💬 Teach It: Coding with a very young child

Coaching a five-year-old is different from coaching an older child. Their attention comes in short bursts, their fingers are still learning to drag, and their imagination is huge. Plan around all three.

Keep sessions short: 10–15 minutes

Stop while it's still fun, ideally right after a big laugh. Ending on a high means they'll ask to do it again tomorrow. Two short sessions a week will get you further than one long, tearful one.

Tell the story first, then code it

Before touching the tablet, ask for a tiny story out loud: who is in it and what happens. Then turn each thing that happens into a block.

“So the puppy jumps, gets really big, and barks. Which picture looks like jumping? …Yes, the hop! What comes next in your story?”

You read, they tap

If there's anything to read, like a menu or a Say bubble, you read it aloud. They tap, drag, and press the green flag. Keep your hands in your lap. If a drag keeps missing, steady the tablet rather than doing it for them.

Let them record silly sounds

The microphone is the most motivating feature in the whole app. Burps, animal noises, a sibling's name, “I'm a dinosaur!”: let them record whatever they like. Hearing their own voice come out of a character they coded is pure magic.

Celebrate out loud

“You made the puppy do that! You told it what to do, and it listened. That's what coders do.”

👶 Ages 4–5

Sit them on your lap and treat it as a shared picture book. You set up the character; they choose the blocks from two or three you point to and do all the tapping. One trigger plus two or three blocks is plenty. Recording sounds is the whole show.

👧 Ages 5–6

They drag and snap on their own while you sit beside them. Build “My Pet Says Hi” together, then invite changes: “What else could it do when you tap it?” Introduce the grid by counting squares together for Move blocks.

🧒 Ages 6–7

Give a goal, not steps: “Make a story with two pages.” Let them draw characters, snap in their own photo, and try Repeat or Start on Bump. Ask them to explain their row to you, left to right, like reading a sentence.

🌟 End with a show

Use full-screen mode and have them “perform” their story for the family, tapping the pet on cue. Clapping and asking for an encore beats any sticker chart.

🎓 When to move up to Scratch

There's no graduation date. Most kids are ready to try full Scratch at around 7 or 8, but readiness matters more than age. Look for these signs:

  • Reading comfort. They can sound out short block labels like “move 10 steps” without getting frustrated.
  • They want more. They ask for things ScratchJr can't do, like keeping score or making a game with rules.
  • They're outgrowing four pages. Their stories keep bumping into the limits.
  • They can use a mouse or trackpad, since Scratch is mostly built for a computer.

You don't have to switch all at once. Many kids happily use both for a while. When you do move over, point out what carries across: “Remember the yellow start blocks? In Scratch they're called Events. And instead of a row, the blocks stack up like a tower.” Then begin with Lesson 1.3's moving, talking cat, which is the same idea as their pet.

📓 Learning Journal

Take five minutes to write it down

A notebook, a notes app, or a sticky note on the fridge all work. Writing it down helps it stick, and your notes will help you plan the next session with your little one.

  1. Key concepts: In your own words, how is a ScratchJr row like a Scratch stack, and how is it different? Which of the six block colors would your child use most?
  2. What clicked: Was there a moment when ScratchJr made a coding idea feel simpler, like counting grid squares or hearing a recorded voice come out of a character?
  3. Questions & confusion: Did anything trip you up, such as the mismatched colors, pages, or finding the microphone? Write it down so you can check it before your child sits down.
  4. Ideas to try: Jot down one tiny story your child could turn into a two-page ScratchJr project. Who's in it, and what happens?
  5. Progress & feelings: How did it feel to hand the tablet to a very young child and keep your hands in your lap? What made them laugh?

💡 Teach It tip: Young kids can journal too. Ask them to draw their pet and what it did, and write their words underneath for them. Months later, those drawings are a wonderful record of how they grew.

Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • ScratchJr is a free tablet app (iPad, Android, Chromebook) for ages 5–7 that uses picture blocks, so no reading is needed.
  • Blocks snap into a horizontal row read left to right, in six colors: yellow Triggering, blue Motion, purple Looks, green Sound, orange Control, and red End.
  • Projects can have up to four pages, and Go to Page turns them like a picture book. Projects stay on the device, with no public community.
  • Coach little ones in 10–15-minute sessions: story first, you read, they tap, lots of silly sounds and celebration.
  • Move up to Scratch at around 7–8, when reading is comfortable and they want more than ScratchJr offers.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

My child is 4. Is that too young for ScratchJr?

Not if you're sitting together. ScratchJr is designed for 5–7, but plenty of 4-year-olds enjoy it on a parent's lap. Keep it very short, focus on tapping and recording sounds, and don't worry about “finishing” anything. If it isn't fun yet, put it away for a few months and try again.

We don't have a tablet. Can we use ScratchJr on a laptop?

ScratchJr is built for tablets and Chromebooks. If you only have a regular computer, you can still use full Scratch with a young child. Just follow the Ages 5–7 coaching tips in each lesson: you read and drag if needed, and they choose and press the green flag.

My 6-year-old has an older sibling on Scratch and wants to use that instead. Should I say no?

No need. Let them try Scratch next to their sibling; if it frustrates them, offer ScratchJr as “your own coding app.” Kids often move back and forth. The concepts are the same, so time in either one counts.
