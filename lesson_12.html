🌠 Lesson 4.3: Capstone — Plan & Build a Game Together

This is the big one. You and your child will plan a game on paper, then build Star Catcher in Scratch. Stars rain down, you catch them in a bowl, and you have three lives. It uses nearly everything you've learned: loops, if/then, variables, broadcasts, clones, and your own My Blocks.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Plan a game before coding it: brainstorm, pick one idea, sketch it, and list the pieces
  • Fill in a one-page game design card together with your child
  • Build the smallest playable version first, then add features one at a time
  • Combine clones, a My Block, variables, and a broadcast into one finished game
  • Split a long project across sittings and share the work fairly with your child

Estimated Time: 60 minutes (best split into two or three sittings)  •  Project: Star Catcher, a complete game with score, lives, and Game Over

Ages 5–7 (with help) Ages 8–11 Ages 12+

🎬 What we're building

Star Catcher works like this. Stars fall from random spots at the top of the screen. You slide a bowl left and right with the arrow keys. Each star you catch adds a point to your score. Each star that hits the ground costs you one of your three lives. When the lives run out, a big GAME OVER appears and everything stops.

Here's where each thing you've learned shows up:

Game pieceIdea it usesWhere you learned it
Bowl moves with arrow keysforever + if + key pressedLesson 2.3
Score and livesVariablesLesson 3.2
Lots of falling starsClonesLesson 4.1
One block that resets the gameMy BlocksLesson 4.2
Game Over appearsBroadcastsLesson 3.1

But the most important skill in this lesson isn't a block at all. It's planning. That comes first.

🎓 Learn It: Plan before you code

When kids start a game by opening Scratch and dragging blocks, it usually ends in one of two ways. Either the game gets too big to finish, or nobody is quite sure what it's supposed to do. Five minutes of planning on paper prevents both. Here's the routine:

  1. Brainstorm. Toss out lots of game ideas with no judging. Silly ones are welcome ("a taco that dodges forks!").
  2. Pick ONE small idea. Choose something with one main action: catch, dodge, jump, or chase. Save the other ideas on a "someday" list so nobody feels their idea was thrown away.
  3. Sketch it on paper. Draw the stage. Where does the player start? What moves? How do you score? How do you win or lose?
  4. List the pieces as a checklist. Every sprite, every variable, every rule becomes a line you can tick off. That list is your to-do list.
  5. Build the smallest playable version first. Get the core action working, even if it's plain. Then add one thing, test it, and add the next.

🌱 Growth mindset: the messy middle is normal

Every big project has a stretch where it's half-built, a bit broken, and nobody is sure it will ever work. Game designers call this "the messy middle," and it's normal. It doesn't mean you're bad at this. It means you're partway through. When you hit it, say it out loud: "This is the messy part. We just need to get one more piece working." Your child will learn that big things get finished one small piece at a time, and they'll learn it from watching how you handle the messy part.

🎓 Learn It: The game design card

Here's a one-page card you and your child can fill in together. Copy it onto paper or an index card, or print this page. The left column is the question. The right column shows how we filled it in for Star Catcher. When your child designs their own game later, they'll write their own answers there.

🃏 Game Design Card

QuestionOur answer (Star Catcher)
Game nameStar Catcher
Who is the player?A bowl at the bottom of the screen
Controls: how do you move?Left and right arrow keys
What else moves?Stars fall from random spots at the top
How do you score?Catch a star: +1 point and a "pop" sound
How do you lose?A star hits the ground: lose 1 of 3 lives
What happens at the end?GAME OVER appears and the game stops
Smallest playable versionBowl moves + stars fall + catching adds score
Someday list (later ideas)Faster stars, rocks to dodge, high score, levels

✏️ Sketch box: leave a big empty rectangle at the bottom of the paper card. Draw the stage with the bowl at the bottom, a few stars at the top, and arrows showing which way things move.

From the card, write the checklist of pieces. For Star Catcher that's: a Bowl sprite, a Star sprite, a Game Over sprite, a score variable, a lives variable, a reset game block, and a game over broadcast. Seven things. Seven ticks. That's a game you can finish.

💡 Why "someday list" matters

Kids hate having ideas rejected. A someday list lets you say, "Great idea! Let's put it on the someday list and add it after the main game works." The idea gets kept, and it's also the first thing to do in Part 3.

🛠️ Try It: Build Star Catcher

Follow these steps in order, and press the green flag after each one. Time estimates are for a parent working alone. With a child in the driver's seat, expect them to take roughly twice as long, and that's fine. The first seven steps give you the smallest playable version. Steps 8–9 add lives and Game Over.

  1. Set the stage (about 4 min)

    Start a new project. Delete the cat: right-click it in the sprite list and choose delete. Use Choose a Sprite to add a Bowl and a Star from the library. Pick a night-sky backdrop (try "Stars" or "Galaxy"). Drag the bowl near the bottom of the stage.

  2. Make the variables (about 3 min)

    In Variables, click Make a Variable. Name it score and choose For all sprites. Do the same again for lives. Leave both checkboxes ticked so they show on the stage.

  3. Build the reset game block on the Bowl (about 6 min)

    Select the Bowl. In My Blocks, click Make a Block, type reset game, and click OK. A pink define hat appears. Snap these under it:

    define reset game
    set score to 0
    set lives to 3
    go to x: 0 y: -150
    show

    This is the same reset game block from Lesson 4.2. Everything that "starts a fresh game" now lives in one named block. When you add more things later, like a speed or a level, you'll add them here.

  4. Make the Bowl move (about 6 min)

    Still on the Bowl, build the green-flag script. It calls reset game once, then checks the arrow keys forever:

    when ⚑ clicked
    reset game
    forever
    if key right arrow pressed? then
    change x by 10
    if key left arrow pressed? then
    change x by -10

    Click the green flag, then the stage, and tap the arrows. The bowl slides, and score and lives show 0 and 3.

  5. Turn the Star into a star factory (about 5 min)

    Select the Star. The original star never falls. It hides and keeps making clones, like a factory behind the curtain:

    when ⚑ clicked
    hide
    wait 1 seconds
    forever
    create clone of myself
    wait pick random 0.5 to 1.5 seconds

    The one-second wait gives the player a moment to get ready. The random wait means the stars don't arrive in a predictable rhythm.

  6. Make each clone fall (about 6 min)

    Still on the Star, start a second script with when I start as a clone. Each new clone jumps to a random spot along the top, appears, and falls:

    when I start as a clone
    go to x: pick random -220 to 220 y: 170
    show
    forever
    change y by -5

    Press the green flag. Stars rain down! They pile up at the bottom for now, and we'll fix that in step 8.

  7. Catch the stars (about 5 min)

    Inside the clone's forever loop, under change y by -5, add a catching check. For the sound, open the Star's Sounds tab and add Pop from the library, or use any sound you like.

    when I start as a clone
    go to x: pick random -220 to 220 y: 170
    show
    forever
    change y by -5
    if touching Bowl? then
    change score by 1
    hide
    play sound Pop until done
    delete this clone

    Why hide and play sound … until done instead of just start sound? Deleting a clone also silences its sound, so the pop would get cut off. Hiding first makes the star vanish right away, and waiting for the sound to finish lets you hear the whole pop before the clone is removed.

    🎉 Smallest playable version: done!

    Stop and play it. You have a real game: stars fall, you catch them, and the score goes up. If you're splitting this lesson across sittings, this is a good place to stop. Everything after this is an addition.

  8. Missing a star costs a life (about 5 min)

    Add a second if inside the same forever loop, under the first one. When a clone falls below the catch zone, it takes a life and deletes itself. If that was the last life, it announces Game Over:

    when I start as a clone
    go to x: pick random -220 to 220 y: 170
    show
    forever
    change y by -5
    if touching Bowl? then
    change score by 1
    hide
    play sound Pop until done
    delete this clone
    if y position < -170 then
    change lives by -1
    if lives = 0 then
    broadcast game over
    delete this clone

    The green less than block comes from Operators; drop the blue y position reporter (bottom of Motion) into its left slot. To make the game over message, open the broadcast block's dropdown and choose New message.

  9. Show GAME OVER (about 5 min)

    Click Paint (the brush under Choose a Sprite) to make a new sprite. Use the Text tool to write GAME OVER in big letters and name the sprite Game Over. Give it two scripts:

    when ⚑ clicked
    hide
    when I receive game over
    go to front layer
    show
    stop all

    stop all freezes everything and removes all the clones, but the Game Over sprite stays visible because it was shown first. If you'd rather use a backdrop, paint a "Game Over" backdrop and use switch backdrop to in the Stage instead. Just remember to switch back to the sky at the start of each game.

  10. Playtest and tune (about 5 min)

    Play three full games. Too hard? Change -5 to -3 so stars fall slower, or make the wait between stars longer. Too easy? Do the opposite. Changing numbers until the game feels right is what game designers call tuning, and kids love doing it.

✅ Project Completion

Tick these off together. When every box is checked, you've finished a real game.

  • ☐ The bowl moves left and right with the arrow keys
  • ☐ The green flag resets score to 0 and lives to 3 (via reset game)
  • ☐ The original star stays hidden; clones fall from random spots
  • ☐ Catching a star adds 1 to score and plays a sound
  • ☐ A missed star removes 1 life and disappears
  • ☐ At 0 lives, GAME OVER appears and everything stops
  • ☐ Pressing the green flag again starts a fresh game
  • ☐ The project is saved (File → Save now, or Save to your computer)

🛠️ Try It: Make it theirs

Now bring out the someday list. Let your child pick one item, add it, test it, and only then pick another.

🌟 If you want to go further: stars speed up over time

Make a speed variable (For all sprites). In reset game, add set speed to 3. In the clone script, replace change y by -5 with change y by (0 − speed) using the green subtraction block. Then, next to change score by 1, add change speed by 0.2. Every catch makes the next stars a little faster.

🌟 If you want to go further: rocks that cost a life

Right-click the Star and choose duplicate, then change the new sprite's costume to a rock. In the rock's clone script, change the touching Bowl part to change lives by -1 (plus the same if lives = 0 → broadcast game over check) instead of adding score. A rock that reaches the bottom just deletes itself, with no penalty. Now players have to dodge as well as catch.

🌟 If you want to go further: a high score

Make a high score variable. In the Game Over sprite, before stop all, add: if score > high score then set high score to score. Don't put it in reset game, because the point is that it survives between games. If you save the project, the high score is saved with it.

🌟 If you want to go further: levels

Make a level variable, set to 1 in reset game. In the Bowl's forever loop, add: if score = 10 and level = 1 then set level to 2, switch backdrop, broadcast level up. Other sprites can react to level up by going faster or changing color. For level 3, use the same pattern with a bigger number.

What you seeLikely causeFix
A star sits frozen at the top and never fallsThe original star is visibleMake sure the green-flag script on the Star starts with hide; only clones should show
No stars appear at allThe clone script is missing show, or it's on the wrong spritePut when I start as a clone on the Star and include show
Stars pile up at the bottomThe y position < -170 check is missing or outside the foreverPut it inside the clone's forever loop, with delete this clone inside the if
Lives drop by more than 1 per missdelete this clone isn't inside the lives ifPut delete this clone as the last block inside that if
Catch sound is cut off or silentThe clone is deleted while its sound is still playingUse hide → play sound … until done → delete this clone
GAME OVER never showsThe broadcast and receive messages don't match, or lives never reach exactly 0Pick the same game over message in both blocks; check lives starts at 3 in reset game
Score doesn't reset on replayreset game isn't called, or it's called inside the foreverPut reset game right under the Bowl's green flag, before the forever

Test after every step. If it worked a minute ago and doesn't now, the bug is almost certainly in the last thing you added.

💬 Teach It: Build it as a team

A capstone is the first project where your child can really own the decisions, even if they can't do every drag and drop yet. Set up the roles before you start.

The child directs; the parent types when needed

Your child is the director. They decide what the game is, what the sprites look like, what happens next, and whether a change is "right." You're the typist when needed: you take over the mouse only for fiddly bits, and only when they ask. Say it out loud at the start:

"You're the boss of this game. I'm your helper. Tell me what you want, and if you want me to do a tricky part, just say 'your turn.'"

When you do take the mouse, narrate what you're doing and hand it back as soon as you're done:

"Okay, I'm finding 'change x by'... it's in blue Motion... snapping it inside the if. Your turn — press the flag and see if it worked!"

Planning out loud

Use the design card as a conversation, not a form. Ask the questions and write their answers in their words:

"If you explained this game to your friend, what would you say? How do you win? How do you lose? What's the one thing the player does most?"

Keep a 60-minute project fun

  • Split it into sittings. Sitting 1: plan and design card, plus steps 1–4 (the bowl moves). Sitting 2: steps 5–7 (smallest playable version). Sitting 3: lives, Game Over, and one someday item.
  • Stop on a win. End each sitting right after something works, not in the middle of a bug. Next time will start with a good feeling.
  • Play breaks count. Every time a step works, play the game for a minute. That's the reward.
  • Watch for tiredness. Short answers, flopping on the chair, or "you do it" usually mean it's time to save and stop.

👧 Ages 5–7

They draw the design card (pictures are fine!), choose the sprites and sounds, and press every green flag. You do most of the dragging while they tell you "left" and "right." Skip lives if it's getting long. Catching stars with a score is a complete game at this age.

🧒 Ages 8–11

They fill in the card and do most of the building, using the steps as a map. You read the next step aloud and help with nesting blocks inside ifs. Let them choose one someday item, like speed-up or rocks.

🧑 Ages 12+

Give them the design card and the checklist, not the steps. Let them work out the scripts and check with you only when stuck. Challenge them to design their own game with the same planning routine once Star Catcher is done.

🌟 End on a high: launch day

When the checklist is complete, hold a "launch." Let your child pick a player, like a sibling, grandparent, or you, and watch them play. Ask your child to explain the rules first, just like on the design card. Seeing someone else enjoy their game is the best reward in all of Scratch.

🎯 Quick Check

Question 1: Your child has a huge game idea with dragons, shops, and ten levels. What's the best first move?

Question 2: In Star Catcher, why does the original Star sprite hide and only make clones?

Question 3: What's the job of the reset game My Block?

📓 Learning Journal

Take five minutes to write it down

This is a milestone lesson, so it deserves a proper entry. Tape your game design card into your notebook next to it.

  1. Key concepts: In your own words, what is the "smallest playable version," and why build it before anything else?
  2. What clicked: Which piece surprised you by how well it fit, such as the hidden star factory, the reset game block, or the Game Over broadcast?
  3. Questions & confusion: Which part of Star Catcher could you not yet rebuild from memory? Write it down so you can try it again later.
  4. Ideas to try: What's on your family's someday list? Which item would you add first, and what blocks do you think it needs?
  5. Progress & feelings: How did the director/typist roles feel? Where was it hard not to grab the mouse, and what did your child do when you held back?

💡 Teach It tip: Have your child glue their design card into their coder's notebook and add a screenshot or drawing of the finished game with their best score. It's the first page of their game design portfolio.

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Plan before you code: brainstorm, pick one small idea, sketch it, and list the pieces as a checklist.
  • A game design card turns a vague idea into a to-do list, and a someday list keeps big ideas without letting them take over.
  • Build the smallest playable version first, then add one feature at a time and test after each.
  • Star Catcher combines clones (falling stars), a My Block (reset game), variables (score, lives), and a broadcast (game over).
  • Your child directs; you type only when needed. Split long projects into sittings and stop on a win.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

We didn't finish in one sitting. Did we do it wrong?

Not at all. Sixty minutes is a lot of focus for anyone, and especially for a child. Two or three shorter sittings is the plan, not a fallback. Save the project, stop right after something works, and pick it back up next time by playing what you've built so far.

My child wants to make a totally different game instead of Star Catcher. Is that okay?

Yes, and that's a great sign. Use the same planning routine: design card, checklist, smallest playable version. Many "different" games are close cousins of this one. Dodging falling things, catching things, and collecting coins all use the same clone-and-touching pattern, so everything here still applies.

I ended up doing most of the building. Is that a problem?

For a first capstone, and especially with younger kids, it's common. What matters is who made the decisions. If your child chose the idea, the sprites, the sounds, and the tuning, it's their game. Next time, try handing over one more step than you did this time. That's exactly what the next lesson is about.
